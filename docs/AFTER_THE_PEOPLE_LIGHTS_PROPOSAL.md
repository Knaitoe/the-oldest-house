After the People Lights Have Gone Off

A vignette design for This House Has Ghosts in It

Design proposal, 2 November 2026. Based on the title story in Stephen Graham Jones’s After the People Lights Have Gone Off, supplied by the user. This is a separate adaptation from A Place for You.

All sample dialogue below is newly written. The visitor, the playable sequence, and the alternative resolutions are additions to the source. This document proposes a design; it does not describe implemented behavior.

The premise

You arrive as a houseguest on the evening Kelly is considering accepting her father’s offer to buy the house.

Her husband Mark works upstairs. Kelly lives mostly downstairs. Above Mark’s study is the loft where their married life in this house began, and where Kelly fell before there were railings. Her father Stan built the house. All three have reasons to love it, blame it, defend it, and want it gone.

Before the night is over, you will hear movement upstairs while Kelly is below you, learn the sound of her wheelchair well enough to recognize its absence, and help the couple decide what they want to do together.

The central question is: What does being there for someone actually require, when love, guilt, care, and fear are becoming difficult to distinguish?

The player is a visitor. Mark and Kelly remain the center of their own marriage. You cannot diagnose them, purchase their trust with chores, or choose a spouse’s fate over the other spouse’s objection.

Recommended length: 25–35 minutes, with optional conversation extending it. One evening and one night; a single short transition to morning is available in the source-aligned ending. The player never has to sleep to advance the story.

What the adaptation preserves

|Source element                                             |Playable treatment                                                                                                    |
|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Mark and Kelly’s affectionate, strained marriage           |Begin with their humor and everyday cooperation. Give each private concerns and the ability to disagree.              |
|The unfinished loft, the fall, and Mark sleeping through it|History revealed through conversation and the actual height of the house. No mandatory reenactment of the accident.   |
|Stan’s pride, denial, help, and fear                       |One visit and an open offer. His earlier refusal to adapt the house coexists with his quietly funding the garage ramp.|
|Two wheelchairs and a house split by floors                |One chair downstairs; the spare upstairs. Their locations are ordinary facts before they become unsettling.           |
|Mark recognizing Kelly by the sound of her chair           |Surface sounds let the player learn the same geography.                                                               |
|Kelly moving without her chair while awake                 |An early, ordinary interaction makes this distinct from the later sleeping loop.                                      |
|Repetitive nocturnal crawling                              |A persistent encounter the player can investigate and interrupt.                                                      |
|The standing likeness upstairs                             |A distant, human-looking presence. Its identity and agency remain uncertain.                                          |
|Mark’s guilt and thoughts about exchanging bodily losses   |Conversation and an optional encounter near the stair rail. Never a challenge to injure yourself.                     |
|Kelly asking for another night before calling Stan         |The main decision scene. The couple negotiate their own plan.                                                         |
|The final bodily exchange                                  |The source-aligned route retains its intimacy and horror, with a restrained presentation and a playable coda.         |

The guest role compresses events that unfold over a longer period in the source. By the time you arrive, the accident, the first sleep movements, and Stan’s offer have happened. You enter an existing marriage under pressure.

The source ending is Mark’s loving, disturbing interpretation of what is happening to him. Preserve that uncertainty. His tenderness does not establish that Kelly consciously chose the exchange, or that this is an uncomplicated act of informed sacrifice.

The people

Kelly

Kelly is introduced doing something she has chosen: preparing supper, selecting music, or sorting things she wants to take if they move. She is funny, sometimes impatient, and capable of making an evening pleasant even when the house is difficult.

Her wishes are specific. She wants the mirror brought within her view. She wants Mark to stop treating every silence as an emergency. She also wants help with some things, and can ask for it directly.

She can want to leave while grieving this house. She can want to stay without being deluded or secretly monstrous. Missing the life they expected does not mean rejecting the life she has.

Use her name and ordinary seated body language. No ominous introduction that teaches the player to treat her wheelchair as a warning.

Sample opening:

> Kelly: The bathroom mirror needs lowering. Mark’s been planning it for three weeks.
> 
> Mark: I was going to do it after work.
> 
> Kelly: Good. It’s after work.

She smiles at him when she says it. He knows the joke. Let the affection land.

Mark

Mark is tired and observant. He can tell where Kelly is by a change in the chair’s sound. At first that seems like the unremarkable knowledge of someone who lives with another person.

He also listens when she has not asked him to. He removes things that quiet the floor. He thinks being constantly alert might make up for the night he slept through her fall.

Do not reduce him to a villain or an exemplary caregiver. He can know exactly how Kelly likes her tea and still fail to ask what she wants now.

He admits the accident in pieces. The player should not have to select a cruel accusation to learn it.

Sample private exchange:

> Mark: I heard something that night.
> 
> Player: When she fell?
> 
> Mark: I thought it was outside. I didn’t even get out of bed.

Give him a pause. No dramatic sound sting.

Stan

Stan visits before dark. His offer to buy the house is real, practical, and available without a secret condition.

He speaks to Mark as though the two men can solve Kelly’s future between them. Kelly can interrupt. If the player redirects a question to her, Stan can catch himself and answer her directly.

His mixed history matters. He resisted structural changes; he also paid for the garage ramp. He is frightened by Kelly’s nighttime movements because of her mother’s sleepwalking. That history is a fragment of family memory, not a confirmed supernatural explanation.

No diary reveals what he is hiding. He can stop in the middle of a sentence because he does not want to say more.

He leaves an offer on the kitchen table, says he will answer the phone, and goes. That is the only required readable document.

The house as a playable place

Build a narrow, tall, believable home. The important feature is a shared vertical space that makes two people nearby but difficult to reach.

A rough exterior footprint of 15 by 23 blocks is a starting budget, with an attached garage. Adjust it to real actor dimensions rather than squeezing the chair into a nominal plan.

|Place               |What belongs there                                                             |What it does in play                                                                                |
|--------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Foyer               |Large floor tiles, newel post, coats, ordinary shoes                           |Introduces the fall’s geometry without a cinematic reconstruction.                                  |
|Living room         |Wood floor, sofa, lamp, a shared photograph                                    |Lets the player sit with both spouses; the chair sounds different here.                             |
|Kitchen             |Smaller tiles, worn counter positions, useful items within Kelly’s chosen reach|Establishes a working domestic routine rather than a showroom.                                      |
|Bedroom and bathroom|Ground-floor access, space beside the bed, mirror hanging too high             |Supports the household action and the final night.                                                  |
|Hall                |A runner and clear turning space                                               |Gives silence a location and makes moving the runner consequential.                                 |
|Study               |Work desk, second chair, railing over the ground floor                         |Shows the upstairs/downstairs division and permits an ordinary overhead sound to become frightening.|
|Loft                |Stored boxes, old air mattress, high finished railing                          |Carries the memory of the accident and the standing figure.                                         |
|Garage route        |Existing ramp and usable door                                                  |Makes departure materially possible.                                                                |

The ground-floor route connects bedroom, hall, foyer, living room, and kitchen. It is wide enough for Kelly to travel and turn without the player clearing an obstacle course. No required chair route includes stairs, ladders, jumps, or a squeeze past the player’s body.

The stairs turn against the wall on the way to the study. A ladder connects study to loft. Ground-floor sightlines reach the study rail and only part of the loft.

Let an occupied chair pass a standing visitor. Test corners and door swings with the actual seated actor. The top-floor chair must be legibly a spare chair, not an unexplained duplicate of Kelly.

The garage opens onto a short driveway apron. Stan can meet the couple there in the departure ending. The onward House threshold is distinct from the bedroom, so leaving the vignette never requires entering an intimate scene.

No front-door accessibility puzzle: the existing garage ramp already answers how Kelly gets in and out.

What the player actually does

There are three intertwined activities.

1. Spend time with the couple. Join supper, ask about something you noticed, sit through a difficult pause, and decide whether to share a private concern.
2. Make a chosen household change. Help with the mirror, a reachable object, the hall runner, or bringing part of Mark’s work downstairs.
3. Investigate a discrepancy in the house. Follow a sound or an absence of sound, compare it with what you know, and speak to the people affected.

Only one small household action is needed to establish the player’s role. The others are optional opportunities. Parts are already present; nothing requires leaving the house to fetch a special item.

Every household change needs a human purpose:

• Lower the bathroom mirror to Kelly’s requested height. She decides whether the angle works.
• Move an item Kelly identifies. Do not randomly tidy her arrangements.
• Ask before lifting the hall runner. Kelly may prefer its quiet; Mark may want to hear the chair.
• Move the small work case downstairs when Mark agrees. Bringing the entire desk down a spiral stair is unnecessary.
• Walk the garage route with Kelly when she wants to check the evening’s departure plan.

The mirror can be an authored prop with a changed height and angle. A new live-reflection renderer is not necessary to make the action understandable.

These actions can affect later staging and conversation. They never add up to an exorcism.

Scene 1: Supper before dark

The front approach offers a view into a warmly lit house. Let the player hear a short, ordinary disagreement before meeting anyone.

Kelly welcomes the visitor. Mark comes down from the study. Neither is waiting motionless for a quest acceptance.

At the table, three subjects can emerge naturally:

• Stan’s offer and whether accepting it feels like giving up.
• Mark’s work upstairs.
• Something about the house that Kelly still loves.

The loft story is available if the player asks about the air mattress, the unusual height, or why they sleep downstairs. If the player does not ask, Mark later volunteers enough to make the night intelligible.

Kelly can tell part of the accident herself. Mark does not own the account simply because he feels guilty.

Stan’s visit overlaps this scene. His offer is visible and concrete: fair purchase, help moving, a place they can use. Let Kelly ask him a practical question before anyone argues about what the house means.

Departure is possible from the beginning. The garage door does not become usable only when the story permits an ending.

Scene 2: The ordinary house

Kelly asks for the mirror adjustment, or accepts another specific offer of help. Mark needs to finish a short call upstairs.

The player gets an unhurried period to move between rooms. A single pass with Kelly teaches the chair sounds:

|Surface         |Ordinary sound                                     |
|----------------|---------------------------------------------------|
|Foyer tile      |Small, spaced wheel knocks at the joints           |
|Living-room wood|A softer continuous roll with a few familiar creaks|
|Kitchen tile    |A closer, lighter rhythm                           |
|Hall runner     |Much quieter wheel movement                        |

This is spatial information, not a rhythm code. Subtitles can name the location and kind of sound after it has been learned. A player without directional audio must still be able to follow.

One encounter shows Kelly awake outside the chair. She has moved to the floor beside the sofa for an ordinary reason; the chair is close. She responds immediately and clearly.

> Kelly: Could you pass the cushion?
> 
> Player: Do you want a hand getting back?
> 
> Kelly: In a minute. This is comfortable.

She may accept help at the transfer if offered again at the appropriate time. There is no award for refusing assistance or for insisting on providing it.

The later horror must differ through consciousness, repetition, friction, and response. The shape of a disabled body alone cannot carry the scare.

Scene 3: Someone upstairs

The first discrepancy is small: a footstep above the study while Mark is visibly at his desk, or the sound of the loft ladder while both spouses are downstairs.

The player can investigate, ask someone, or ignore it.

A later sighting gives more: an upright silhouette resembling Kelly at the high rail. Familiar hair, clothing, a way of resting a hand. No glowing eyes, attack pose, or sudden creature face.

It is brief but not a one-frame event. A player who turns slowly can still register it. The figure withdraws behind real architecture. A later opportunity or Mark’s testimony can supply the information if it was missed.

Do not label it “Kelly’s ghost” in a nameplate or quest entry. The player has seen a resemblance, not obtained an ontological answer.

If the player tells Kelly:

> Kelly: Was it me?
> 
> Player: It looked like you.
> 
> Kelly: Those aren’t the same answer.

This is a serious question about what was actually witnessed, not a twist in which she reveals secret possession.

Two small bodily clues can accompany this section. Mark keeps rubbing an irritated patch at his hip through his clothes. A scrape Kelly mentions after an earlier night no longer looks as she remembers it. Neither clue requires inspecting a body; either can be conveyed in conversation. They prepare the final exchange without supplying a medical diagnosis or a rule for how it works.

The optional rail encounter

Mark later takes the spare chair near the top of the stairs. His explanation starts as a thought experiment about whether one ruined moment could be exchanged for another.

The scene must allow interruption with speech from a normal approach. Calling his name makes him turn and stop moving. No timed sprint, falling damage, or moral reward for catching him.

If the visitor is farther away, the likeness appears behind him and the chair rolls back from the edge. Mark can describe what he felt afterward. This preserves the source’s disturbing rescue without requiring the player to watch it happen.

If the visitor intervenes first, the scene continues as a conversation. Never force Mark toward the edge again to preserve a scheduled scare.

The point is that his wish to compensate can become dangerous. It is not evidence that injury would make the marriage fair.

Scene 4: The sleeping route

After the evening conversation, Kelly goes to the bedroom. Mark stays up. The player can keep company, explore, or choose a chair in the living room.

This is a scene transition triggered by the couple finishing the conversation, not a real-time requirement to remain idle. The player does not get a mandatory sleep prompt.

Later, the familiar chair sound stops. A quieter scraping takes its place.

Kelly is asleep and moving around the ground-floor loop without the chair. She repeats the turn around the newel post. The motion is effortful and recurrent. Her face is slack; she does not answer normally.

The loop persists long enough to investigate. Looking away does not secretly fail an objective. Mark can draw attention to it if the player has wandered upstairs.

Use a small amount of visible friction: cloth catching, an arm finding the same joint in the floor, a sound that repeats in the same place. Avoid a combat creature animation, pursuit music, or extensive injury effects.

The player can respond sensibly:

|Action                          |Consequence                                                                                                                 |
|--------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Call Kelly’s name               |With repetition and Mark’s help, she wakes. The loop stops for this scene. She is disoriented and has no useful explanation.|
|Turn on a lamp                  |The route becomes easier to see. Light does not banish the likeness or automatically wake someone.                          |
|Ask Mark what he has seen before|He admits the pattern and shows the bandaged hand from an earlier attempt to wake her.                                      |
|Approach closely                |Opens an interaction; it never automatically grabs, carries, or restrains Kelly.                                            |
|Move into the route             |She pauses at the obstruction. No collision damage or forced clipping. The player can step away or call to her.             |
|Withdraw                        |The couple address the immediate movement; the player can rejoin their conversation afterward.                              |

Waking Kelly works. It does not cure every future night. If she goes back to sleep later, that happens after a new, explicit conversation with the couple. No immediate reset that makes a sensible intervention feel pointless.

The player can describe what happened without claiming to know its cause. If the player did not see it, dialogue says Mark reported it. Evidence is an account of events, not a camera challenge.

Scene 5: The conversation that changes the night

Regroup at the kitchen table or the ground-floor bedroom doorway. Kelly is awake. The supernatural encounter has not made her a subject for the other two to discuss over her head.

Mark admits what he has been afraid to say: that the movement upstairs looks like the Kelly he remembers, that the sleeping route frightens him, and that he has been trying to keep her within hearing.

Kelly can be angry about the listening and frightened by the missing time at once.

Use a small set of player responses:

• Ask Kelly what she wants tonight.
• Ask Mark what he has been leaving out.
• Suggest calling Stan together.
• Offer a concrete way to spend the night downstairs together.
• Say that you cannot tell them what the house is doing.

No response announces a moral category. The player can acknowledge uncertainty and still help.

The couple’s possible plans are discussed openly. A resolution becomes available when both agree to it in dialogue. There is no hidden affection score or persuasion password.

If one refuses a proposal, that refusal stands for the current conversation. The player can help with another plan, ask what would make a decision practical, or leave. Optional chores never buy a reversal.

The player can always ask for time before committing. No irreversible night event runs while this conversation is open.

Three resolutions

A. One more night — the source-aligned resolution

Kelly wants to call her father in the morning. Mark wants to stay with her tonight. They agree to share the bedroom, and ask whether the visitor will remain until morning.

The player chooses whether to stay. Refusing does not turn either spouse hostile.

If staying, return the scene to small domestic actions: water beside the bed, a lamp, Mark’s work case left downstairs. Mark checks the bedroom door because he has begun thinking of the danger as something outside.

The default staging closes the bedroom door, retaining the source’s mistake about where the danger is. If the couple have instead agreed to leave it partly open while the visitor remains nearby, the glimpse below is available. That sightline is an adaptation choice, not a feature of the source’s locked-bedroom ending. Calling through a closed door can still wake Kelly.

The exchange occurs when Kelly is asleep beside him. Present it through a restrained threshold glimpse if the visitor is still nearby: bedclothes, Mark’s hand against her hair, a narrow strand of shadow whose bodily origin is obscured, her left foot curling, his feet still. His initial alarm gives way to tenderness. The camera remains under player control.

This adds a visitor’s possible sightline to the source. It does not require the guest to enter, expose bodies, or remain as an audience. Do not stage a graphic close-up of the source’s intimate contact.

The scene also works behind a closed door. A quiet exchange of sounds leads to a morning coda, reached through the player’s explicit choice to let the night pass. No actual wait or compulsory bed use.

The threshold beat remains interactable until the visitor chooses to let the night pass, withdraw, or wake Kelly. Its presentation can settle into a quiet held state. There is no short rescue timer concealed inside an optional glimpse.

At dawn, Mark sits beside the bed. Kelly remains in her own chair; she has not been given an instant ability to walk. She can move the foot a little. Mark says his legs feel numb. The coda establishes only what is observable now, not a permanent diagnosis. If he needs the spare chair, the visitor can bring the empty chair downstairs while Kelly stays with him.

The pair sit close. Kelly does not suddenly explain a plan she carried out while asleep. Mark’s understanding may be loving, frightened, resigned, or all three.

Original coda sample:

> Mark: Your foot.
> 
> Kelly: I know.
> 
> Mark: I couldn’t feel mine.
> 
> Kelly: Give me your hand.

Let the last line bring his attention back to her. End on their attention to each other, with the player able to leave.

If the player wakes Kelly during the threshold glimpse, the exchange stops. Do not punish intervention by replaying it immediately. Offer the departure or changed-house resolution from an awake conversation. The complete source-aligned ending is never forced by taking camera or interaction control away.

Carry any already observed bodily change into that conversation. Interrupting the scene does not automatically restore Mark’s sensation or cancel movement already seen in Kelly’s foot. The spare chair is available for Mark if needed; no branch asks him to walk on legs he has just said he cannot feel.

If the visitor leaves before the night passes, record what was actually witnessed and leave the private outcome unknown. Do not award knowledge of the exchange simply for choosing to stay in a dialogue menu.

B. Leave together — an authored alternative

After hearing both spouses, the player helps them agree to call Stan now.

Kelly makes the call, or explicitly asks someone else to. Stan addresses her when he answers. No financial puzzle, missing phone battery, or new locked exit delays departure.

They choose what to take. Kelly names one object; Mark names another. The player can help carry those objects, but this is a short departure scene with items already nearby.

Both move downstairs and out through the established garage route. Kelly uses her chair. Mark walks with her if the exchange has not begun; if it has affected him, he uses the spare chair brought downstairs. Stan meets them on the driveway apron. The scene ends once both have actually left the house; transport continues after a brief transition.

There is no hauling Kelly down stairs. No secret second route becomes necessary.

The loft likeness may remain at the rail as the couple reach the ramp. It does not rush them or prevent departure.

Original exchange:

> Kelly: I wanted us to get to live here.
> 
> Mark: We did.
> 
> Kelly: I know. That isn’t what I mean.
> 
> Mark: Tell me on the way.

End on a future conversation. Leaving does not heal Kelly, erase the marriage’s conflict, or establish that the phenomenon cannot follow.

The house is empty on a later revisit. The ordinary changes the player made remain visible.

C. Share the downstairs — an authored alternative

Both decide that they want to remain in the house for now, but reject spending another night in the same divided routine.

This requires a real agreement, not the player completing all repairs. Kelly chooses which practical change matters tonight. Mark brings his work downstairs and acknowledges that listening for every movement has become a way to manage his own fear.

The player helps with one chosen adjustment. Previously completed work remains completed. A household task must not mysteriously reset to furnish the ending.

No upstairs lock is installed as a supernatural solution. Kelly retains a usable bedroom and exit. If they want a way to call for help, they decide on it together.

The couple finish something small together: a hand of cards, clearing supper, adjusting the lamp beside the mirror. They discuss calling Stan again when they have had time to think.

Above them, there is a quiet movement at the loft rail. The player can notice it. Neither practical work nor a sincere conversation has certified the house safe.

Original final exchange:

> Kelly: Put it where you can work.
> 
> Mark: Here?
> 
> Kelly: Where you can work. You don’t have to sit facing me.

He turns the work case toward the table. She goes on with what she was doing.

End with shared space and chosen privacy. This is a change in how they live together, not a claim that good communication defeats the supernatural.

Personhood tailoring

HOME is the strongest connection to the personhood vignettes. Let the House make a cautious assumption about what shelter, staying, or leaving might mean to this guest, then let the player answer.

|Read                                      |Small variation                                                                                                         |Required limit                                                                  |
|------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|Settled HAVEN                             |Kelly asks whether leaving a loved home would feel like losing part of a life.                                          |She asks; she does not declare the visitor’s history.                           |
|Settled DANGER                            |The visitor can answer Mark’s concern about protecting someone with a line about protection becoming difficult to leave.|Available as an offered interpretation, not an automatic trauma confession.     |
|Settled RUNAWAY                           |Kelly notices the visitor has kept their things close and explicitly offers the garage route.                           |The route is available for every guest. No assumption that staying proves trust.|
|Tentative, uncertain, unknown, or disabled|Use neutral questions about the present house and tonight.                                                              |The complete story and every resolution still work.                             |

AGE can affect whether the couple initially ask the guest for experience or simply for company. Do not invent a marriage, dead partner, illness, or childhood based on age.

Use established forms of address for SELF without turning presentation into an assertion about gender. The visitor can correct an assumption once; the correction persists.

HAIR can support one optional, practical moment of recognition. Do not turn every hair choice into a compulsory scene about intimate grooming.

DRAWN_TO does not need a branch. Mark and Kelly’s marriage remains theirs. Their warmth toward the visitor is hospitality, with no romance meter.

The House body remains consistent with the rest of the mod. This vignette does not rewrite the global profile or make a new personality inference from how the player handles Kelly’s care.

Consequences and persistence

Track facts that change staging or testimony:

• Which people the visitor actually spoke with.
• Whether the accident was discussed.
• Whether the visitor saw a likeness, heard an unexplained sound, or only heard Mark’s account.
• Whether Kelly was awake or asleep during each witnessed movement.
• Which household changes were requested, agreed, and completed.
• Whether the runner was moved and whose preference was heard.
• Which current-night plan each spouse explicitly accepted.
• Whether the exchange was witnessed, interrupted, reported, or remained private.
• Whether the visitor stayed to morning or departed earlier.
• The household’s resolved state.

Do not track compassion, bravery, disability acceptance, or deservingness as scores.

A private account can be brief and literal:

• Saw a standing figure above the study while Kelly was downstairs.
• Kelly woke after we called to her. She did not remember the route.
• They called Stan together.
• Mark said his legs were numb in the morning.

Only record the first line if that relationship between events was actually observed. Use “Mark told me” when that is the source of knowledge. Do not certify a demon, cure, or soul transaction.

Give the vignette one completion result for any of its three resolved plans. Optional observations and the private exchange never create a second camera-proof completion requirement. Fit any existing Witness quota through its existing derivation rather than inventing another mandatory observation counter.

The overnight plan can resolve when the visitor gives the couple privacy and departs. The household’s private night then proceeds; the visitor’s account leaves its outcome unknown until a later conversation or revisit. Staying to watch or reaching the morning coda is never required for completion. If the visitor interrupts before departing, reopen the current plan and resolve the eventual outcome under the same single completion identity.

Use the mod’s literature companion convention to credit the source title and author alongside the vignette’s account or collection entry. That credit is separate from Stan’s in-world offer. It should not arrive as a new diary purporting to explain the house.

A later visit reflects the actual ending: empty house after departure, rearranged downstairs after the shared-space resolution, or a quiet aftermath after the source-aligned night. No reset that returns Mark upstairs while declaring he has already moved down.

Staging and implementation boundaries

• Use a small cast of distinct human actors: Mark, Kelly, Stan, and an unlabelled likeness. Kelly’s awake, sleeping, seated, and floor states retain her appearance.
• Give the chair audible weight and a believable turning circle. It is an ordinary mobility aid, not an enemy vehicle.
• Use real connected rooms and reachable actor destinations. Do not solve an inaccessible floor by teleporting Kelly between sightings.
• The likeness can be supernatural. Its appearances still need intentional occlusion and a legible withdrawal.
• No required player carrying sequence. The historical upstairs carries can be discussed.
• Keep light useful for seeing. Do not invent a light-powered soul mechanism to literalize the title.
• Do not require a darkness resource, held-item ray, exact eye target, or timed camera position.
• Keep the sleeping loop available while the player investigates. Pause consequential transitions during choice text.
• All essential evidence has speech, subtitle, or observable-state alternatives.
• Keep dialogue in chat for this Ghosts Set story, consistent with the user’s accepted presentation. Avoid interleaving unrelated NPC conversations.
• Use vanilla-adjacent materials and useful household props. The horror comes from familiar rooms and people occupying them differently.
• Protect the authored structure as a whole. Do not let general room decoration or labyrinth generation overwrite the circulation route.
• Never take player inventory or companion custody to stage the guest role. Place companions outside tight bedside positions when needed, and reunite them through the normal route.
• Keep a single agreed household state in multiplayer. Visitors can observe separately; the owning player’s conversation commits the couple’s plan. A second player looking elsewhere does not invalidate a witnessed event.
• Future interaction with the couple must honor their state. Do not replay the transfer as a farming animation.

A practical first build

The complete first version needs the house, the three named people, the silhouette, one requested household change, the surface sounds, the interruptible night route, the joint decision scene, and three short resolutions.

Author the mirror and downstairs work case before adding many optional chores. The two make the house usable and dramatize its division with minimal clutter.

The rail encounter is valuable but optional. Add it after the core night and endings work. Do not make an elaborate rescue animation a prerequisite for testing the relationship.

Verification should cover one ordinary playthrough of each ending, then the cases most likely to break the story:

1. The visitor never looks upstairs.
2. The visitor misses Stan’s visit.
3. Kelly is woken immediately.
4. The visitor leaves a conversation open during the night loop.
5. The visitor declines every optional household task.
6. The visitor asks for the garage exit at arrival.
7. A proposed plan is refused by one spouse.
8. The visitor wakes Kelly during the exchange.
9. The visitor leaves before morning and later returns.
10. Two visitors occupy a doorway, with a companion nearby.
11. Directional audio is unavailable.
12. A resolved house is revisited.

The vignette works when the player remembers a marriage and a specific house, and can explain what they chose to help with. Its supernatural explanation can remain unsettled.
