# A Little Longer (the owner's proposal)

Recorded verbatim, 2 November 2026: the owner's design for an original private romantic vignette. Not built. The owner asked
whether "the chapters could register as different visits?": yes. Like the other multi-visit vignettes, each chapter boundary
would be a resumable visit, with Rowan's state and what really happened between them carried in the reader's own record. It
comes after everything before it in proposal order (the Fuse Board, the Header Tank, A Place for You, Second Person and The
Ones Already Inside). Nothing below is edited; the owner's line breaks, lists and headings are kept as sent.

A Little Longer

An original private romantic vignette for This House Has Ghosts in It

Design proposal, 2 November 2026. Not implemented.

The experience is an evening with someone whose company becomes worth prolonging. The player and that person begin to imagine a next day together. The House forcibly separates them while that future is still an ordinary assumption.

Recommended length: 35–50 minutes, governed by activity and conversation rather than an idle timer. Chapters support leaving and resuming. A reader can spend longer in company. There is no mandatory sleep.

The dramatic center

Two people find a habitable place inside the House. They make something work, eat something unimpressive, get on each other's nerves briefly, and decide they would rather continue together.

The companion is Rowan, an adult who repairs radios. Their shop and the flat above it have become connected to the House. They have been trying to find a way back to their ordinary street.

The reader arrives as a fellow traveler. Rowan has no assigned history with them and makes no claim to have been waiting for them.

The relationship develops through reciprocal attention. Rowan asks for company when the practical reason for staying has ended. They remember what the player did. They reveal an unflattering habit. They initiate affection if it has been welcomed.

The ending is fixed at its center: the House removes Rowan from the reader's reachable world. Rowan resists. The relationship remains genuine.

Player choices determine what the two actually shared, the kind of intimacy they established, the future they had begun planning, and what remains after the cut.

Rowan, before romance

Rowan is competent with small repairs and poor at accepting that some problems will not respond to another ten minutes of work.

They like terrible late-night radio advertisements, dislike raisins in almost anything, and hum the same few notes when concentrating. They usually do not realize they are doing it.

They talk confidently about directions, then quietly check the map. They can laugh when caught.

When frightened, they become brisk and dismissive. They catch themselves afterward and apologize in concrete terms.

They have a normal future they want back: a shop counter that is finally theirs, a kettle they brought from their last job, and a plan to close early on Fridays. These details are ambitions, not an invitation to solve their entire life.

They can enjoy being alone. The reader becomes someone they specifically want beside them.

Do not write Rowan as continuously admiring, endlessly patient, or grateful for every action. They can disagree about a route, want the music left on, or insist that they are not going to let the player call burnt bread a meal.

Their warmth should include initiative. They can bring the second cup without being asked, ask the reader to remain, and admit that the request has stopped being practical.

Casting and personhood

Rowan is one authored person with a stable voice, habits, limits, and wants. Casting variants can use adult bodies and presentation appropriate to a settled DRAWN_TO read. Fix the cast at first meeting and preserve it.

For BOTH, either authored cast can be selected and retained for that story. For unknown reads, do not assume attraction; the same person offers company and the reader can initiate interest. NO_ONE receives a complete companionship route, with present choices able to correct a previous read.

AGE can inform an appropriate adult casting variant. It does not create a dead spouse, children, a past marriage, or a life history for the reader.

SELF affects established address and the handling of the reader's presentation. It does not determine a romantic role or who initiates.

HAIR can support one practical callback, such as offering a cloth or moving a routine object within reach. It should not turn the entire evening into a care ritual.

HOME can change the first question about staying or leaving. Rowan still answers as Rowan, not as a different personality generated to match the reader.

Only use permitted, sufficiently grounded observations. Unknown, uncertain, tentative, and opted-out states have neutral writing as appropriate. None limits the available story.

There are no affection points or profile-based romance requirements. The game records explicit invitations, answers, shared acts, and welcomed gestures.

The place

The set consists of a small repair shop, the flat above it, a roof landing, and a short stretch of the House's adjoining corridor.

The shop is personal: sorted parts, a repaired lamp, an unfinished work order, a chair whose leg Rowan has fixed twice. Objects have owners and uses.

The flat contains a small kitchen table, two mismatched cups, a comfortable chair, an ordinary bathroom, a coat hook, and a window with rain against it. The visible street beyond is an authored backdrop with no reachable normal-world escape.

The roof landing gives the pair a change of space. It has enough shelter to stand out of the rain and enough room for both actors to move naturally.

This place should become recognizable through use. After the severance, the reader must know precisely which chair is empty, where Rowan had been standing, and what the missing sound was.

No decorative mansion, piles of explanatory notes, or gallery of tragic former lovers.

What draws the reader in

The evening needs several distinct forms of closeness.

Cooperation: something works better because both people were present.

Amusement: a shared joke develops from an event, then returns naturally.

Reciprocity: Rowan notices and does something for the reader without waiting for a request.

Friction and repair: they disagree, then choose to continue with more understanding.

Preference: Rowan asks for the reader's company after the useful task is over.

Anticipation: they begin planning another ordinary activity together.

Give these room to happen. A compliment followed by a tragic revelation will not substitute for the evening.

The player has several viable styles: direct flirting, dry humor, hesitant interest, quiet company, or friendship. A shy reply can still communicate interest. Silence alone is not treated as consent to a romantic gesture.

Rowan's response should fit what happened. A player who made them laugh can hear the joke returned. A quiet player can find Rowan comfortable sharing an activity without filling every pause.

Chapter 1: A person at the workbench

Target: 4–7 minutes.

The player hears a radio being tuned and someone humming before seeing Rowan.

Rowan is trying to refit its back panel. A loose screw rolls toward the counter edge. The player can stop it, return it, or simply speak.

Opening example:

Rowan: Could you hold that side a second?

The player steadies the panel.

Rowan: There. Thank you.

The radio produces a harsh burst of noise.

Rowan: That sounded expensive.

They switch it off, then look properly at the visitor.

The player can ask where they are, ask about the radio, joke that the repair seems promising, or say they are passing through.

Rowan has already tried several adjoining doors. They show the reader a penciled route with one crossed-out turn.

If the player questions the directions:

Rowan: I said I knew where the stairs were.

Player: You drew them in three places.

Rowan: And one of those was right.

The first task is short, with every needed part already on the bench. It establishes shared effort and Rowan's particular way of handling a mistake. It is not an admission price for affection.

If the player declines to help, Rowan finishes the panel themselves and offers the same opportunity to talk.

The first remembered detail

Track one actual act. The reader may have held the panel steady, waited at a threshold, returned the screw, or moved the lamp so Rowan could see.

Later, Rowan recalls that precise act. Do not praise a kindness the reader never performed.

This is the first indication that Rowan is attending to the reader, rather than delivering a universal romance script.

Chapter 2: Stay until the kettle boils

Target: 7–10 minutes.

The useful work ends. Rowan puts the tool down.

This is the first important invitation:

Rowan: I'm making tea.

Player: Is that part of the repair?

Rowan: No.

A pause.

Rowan: You could stay for it.

Let them wait for an answer. The pause has meaning because the practical excuse is gone.

Upstairs, Rowan gives the reader the uncracked cup, then takes the comfortable chair without thinking. When the reader notices, they concede that their hospitality has limits.

The reader can sit near them, take the opposite seat, remain standing, or help with food. Their position affects staging and the first subject, not a hidden attraction calculation.

A shared meal with a specific imperfection

They make toast or something equally simple from ingredients already present. Rowan scorches one slice while arguing that the heat is perfectly manageable.

The player can rescue it, tease them, offer the better piece, or insist the burnt one is fine.

If the reader accepts the burnt slice:

Rowan: You don't have to be polite.

Player: I'm being brave.

Rowan: That's worse.

The joke can recur later. It should not be repeated on every interaction.

Rowan also does something small for the reader: brings their cup closer, chooses the chair that blocks the draft, or notices that they have been left holding something.

This is ordinary consideration, with no spoken claim that it proves a special connection.

A place for the reader's answer

Questions concern the present: whether to leave music on, whether the reader likes having a plan, what they would do first after getting outside.

The player can answer without inventing a spouse, bereavement, childhood, or trauma. The script does not borrow real personal history.

Rowan answers the same kind of question about themselves. The exchange is reciprocal.

Chapter 3: The radio isn't a clue

Target: 6–9 minutes.

The radio works. A short original tune comes through clearly enough to recognize.

Rowan wants it left on. The reader can enjoy it, ask to turn it down, or worry that the sound could attract something.

A direct disagreement is available:

Player: We should be listening for the doors.

Rowan: We have been listening for the doors.

Player: They keep changing.

Rowan: I know.

Their next answer is sharper than necessary. They busy themselves with the cups.

The player can push the practical concern, ask why the music matters, leave room for the irritation to pass, or apologize for spoiling the moment.

Rowan must also do the work of repair:

Rowan: That came out badly.

Player: What did?

Rowan: I wanted a minute where it was just a broken radio.

That is enough vulnerability. They do not deliver a speech explaining all their loneliness.

The reader can say they wanted the same thing, maintain that the danger is real, or offer a compromise. Rowan can accept a quieter volume while keeping the tune.

The scene establishes that they can misunderstand each other and still choose each other's company.

The initiative changes sides

If interest has been expressed, Rowan makes the next invitation.

Rowan: When you finish that, come outside with me.

Player: Why?

Rowan: Because I want to show you something.

If the reader is flirting:

Rowan: And because you keep finding reasons to stand up.

For a quiet reader, make the invitation simpler. Rowan asks them to bring the cup and stay a little while. Do not make every version witty.

They are allowed to want the reader there.

Chapter 4: Nothing useful for a while

Target: 7–10 minutes.

On the sheltered roof landing, Rowan shows the reader a tiny patch of sky between the wrong buildings.

They have been using it as a reminder that something exists beyond the doors. They are not certain it is a real sky. They still like looking at it.

The two can sit, lean beside the rail, or remain near the doorway. Nobody has to stand on an exact mark.

This section has no objective to complete. Offer conversation, shared silence, an invitation to move closer, or a return indoors. It should feel like chosen time rather than a wait for a hidden timer.

A possible admission:

Rowan: I thought I'd be braver if something like this happened.

Player: You got the lights back on.

Rowan: That was wiring. I'm good at wiring.

The reader can say that it matters, admit to being frightened too, tease them gently, or sit without trying to fix the feeling.

Rowan can notice the actual first act from the workshop here. A small remembered thing can carry more weight than a grand compliment.

Romantic escalation

Write explicit but natural opportunities.

The reader can say they are glad they stopped.

Rowan can answer that they are glad the reader stayed.

Rowan can offer a hand, with time to accept or decline.

A player can ask to sit closer or kiss them.

After welcomed interest, Rowan can ask to kiss the reader.

Stage the gestures at a believable pace. Let a nervous joke interrupt one. Rowan can look away, then come back to what they meant to say.

A possible direct exchange:

Player: Was there really something you wanted to show me?

Rowan: Yes.

Player: The sky?

Rowan: At first.

Keep that earned by the preceding scenes. It should not be Rowan's first flirt.

If a kiss occurs, give the couple more ordinary time afterward. They can return indoors, disagree about the toast again, or wash the cups. The relationship continues beyond the gesture.

The cut does not occur as an immediate consequence of kissing.

Let them stop hiding behind the activity

On returning indoors, Rowan starts to offer another small job, then abandons the excuse.

Rowan: I was going to say there's something wrong with the radio again.

Player: Is there?

Rowan: No. I just don't want you to go yet.

The reader can answer directly, tease them gently, or admit they were hoping for another excuse too.

Player: You could have just said that.

Rowan: I'm trying.

Let that admission stand. Rowan does not immediately hide it under another joke. For a reader who has already asked to remain, use a reciprocal acknowledgment instead of pretending the invitation is new.

The companionship route

A reader who declines romance receives a real answer and continued warmth. The shared evening still matters. Rowan does not become cold, plead for another chance, or die sooner.

The ending records the relationship that existed. The narration never tells a reader they were secretly in love.

Chapter 5: Tomorrow has two people in it

Target: 5–8 minutes.

Something outside has changed. A door has shifted; an unfamiliar strip of flooring has appeared. The pair investigate together, then agree they should leave the flat and continue through the House.

Rowan is willing to go with the reader. They put on their coat and gather ordinary things. They are not rooted to the shop or protecting a secret that requires staying behind.

Before departure, they return to the kitchen for water and food to take.

This is where a future first becomes casual.

The exact conversation depends on their evening. Rowan may propose making the next meal, ask the reader to choose the music next time, or suggest that they take turns with the map.

In the burnt-toast version:

Rowan: What do you want for breakfast?

Player: Something you haven't set on fire.

Rowan: All right. You're making it.

They find a scrap of paper.

Rowan: What do we need?

The player names a simple food or drink. Rowan adds their own. The note contains two people's preferences.

The ordinary implication matters: they are discussing tomorrow as if they expect to share it.

A warmer variant can include:

Player: You already gave me the good cup.

Rowan: You can have it tomorrow too.

Do not stack every tender line into one scene. Choose the callback that belongs to this particular evening.

The present choice

The reader can accept continuing together, admit that the prospect matters to them, make a joke, or ask to remain companions.

Rowan has their own answer. In the romantic route, let it be unmistakable that they want another day with the reader.

They do not promise eternity, ask to be remembered, predict their own loss, or begin a farewell. They expect to leave together.

The breakfast scrap is written during this scene. It is not a posthumous letter or a message Rowan secretly prepared for their disappearance.

Chapter 6: The cut

The radio tune stops halfway through a phrase.

A narrow vertical seam appears through the room. It should look architectural at first: a wrong edge, a strip of depth that does not agree with the space around it.

Rowan notices it. The reader is present and free to move.

The seam passes through the room without respecting where either person stands. A stretch of wall, part of the counter, and Rowan begin to recede into a thinner space.

Rowan reaches for the reader. If they were already holding hands, the contact is broken by the removal itself. There is no point at which the player chooses to let go.

Use one immediate human reaction. They say the reader's offered name, call for them, or simply say "Wait." No long final speech.

For a moment, Rowan is visible in a narrowing lit space. Their hand is still reaching. They are trying to stay.

Then the space is gone.

The cup is still on the table. The kettle continues. Rowan's humming has stopped. Their coat hook is empty.

The reader has lost someone in the middle of an ongoing relationship. The final image is an interrupted effort to remain together.

How the forced loss is presented

The severance belongs to the House's power over space. It is not a test of reaction speed, an equipment check, or a penalty for choosing intimacy.

The player can reach, move toward Rowan, speak, strike the wall, or stand in shock. Those are expressive actions. Do not label an interaction "Save Rowan" when no such branch exists.

There is no hidden rescue selected by looking at the right eye, crossing first, keeping the radio off, or refusing the kiss.

Likewise, there is no reveal that Rowan was a lure or that the evening never happened. Their desire to remain with the reader was real.

Keep the camera under player control. The ending must remain understandable if the reader misses the narrow final glimpse. Rowan's reaction, the interrupted sound, and the changed room establish the loss without an observation quota.

The severance staging must work with the reader beside Rowan, behind them, or near the door. It cannot depend on a failed following animation or a door closing because the player was too slow.

Chapter 7: The room afterward

Do not immediately eject the reader or start another scare.

Leave enough ordinary room for them to move, look, and act.

They can approach the missing space. It opens onto a bare service wall or a short stairwell that has no continuation into Rowan's side of the room. This is a real changed layout, not an invisible boundary beside an accessible actor.

They can sit in the chair, turn off the radio, take the cup if they wish, or leave it where it was.

The breakfast scrap remains. Its text uses the choices from their conversation. One line may still be unfinished.

That is the keepsake: a practical note for a day they expected to have.

The reader can keep it, fold it into an account, or leave it on the table. No gesture is narrated as proof of love, cowardice, closure, or failure.

A closing account can record that there were two cups, which one the reader took, and what they had planned. It must not invent a kiss, a promise, or an emotion.

Possible closing sentence if the reader actually took a cup:

We had made a list for breakfast. I brought one of the cups back.

For other actions, write a different factual ending. Do not reuse the sentence regardless of what happened.

No action earns a numeric bond. These are facts two people share and can recognize.

The player can also disappoint Rowan or leave a disagreement unresolved. Rowan can express that disappointment while still wanting to continue. Do not collapse a relationship into perfect responses or a single rejection.

Pacing the draw

Most of the playable time belongs to the relationship.

Allow Rowan to walk, fetch something, become absorbed in a small task, and then return their attention. Continuous eye contact and nonstop flattering dialogue make an NPC feel performed.

Write a few substantial exchanges with room between them. Optional speech should expand the person, not repeat that they like the player.

Offer one or two ordinary topics Rowan brings up for their own interest. Give the reader a chance to discover what amuses them. Let Rowan react to something that is not about the reader.

The small disagreement matters because warmth afterward becomes a chosen continuation.

Keep ominous material sparse until the couple begins preparing to leave. The House is already strange. Repeated threats, fading photographs, or Rowan hinting that they may not exist would turn the entire evening into anticipation of a twist.

The removal should interrupt a future that had only just become believable.

Departure, saving, and a shorter visit

The reader can leave and resume at chapter boundaries. Rowan's local state, completed conversations, cast, and shared acts persist.

An explicit early departure can produce a shorter encounter. Rowan can ask once whether the reader would like company. If the reader agrees to continue together early, compress the later practical preparations and stage the same severance without fabricating an intimacy that never developed.

The complete evening remains the intended romantic route. Give it plausible invitations and activities, not locked doors that force a player to date someone.

There is no real-world wait between chapters and no required re-entry to manufacture elapsed days. A player returning in another session can experience a genuine reunion; a continuous player receives the natural transition between activities.

After the final cut, re-entry preserves the changed room. Rowan does not respawn for another romance attempt, and a server restart cannot reverse the event.

The vignette gives at most one completion for the run. The final glimpse, a kiss, and a collected keepsake are not independent completion requirements.

Staging and implementation

Use vanilla-adjacent human art with adult proportions. Attraction should come through expression, voice, movement, and familiarity. Avoid exaggerated eyes, constant blushing, heart particles, or a visible affection meter.

Rowan should sit at an angle, sometimes look at the task instead of the player, and make room beside them when inviting closeness.

Offer touch through clear interaction and authored animation. Keep the player camera and movement stable. A kiss can be suggested through Rowan's approach and a short optional transition; it does not require a fragile full-body attachment or a forced camera turn.

The radio tune should be short, original, and recognizable. It plays sparingly and ducks under dialogue. Its interruption at the cut supplies a distinct loss of continuity.

Use actual connected paths for Rowan's movement. The player should not develop affection around an actor who repeatedly teleports, clips through furniture, or runs to catch up.

Make the roof safe under ordinary movement. A fall should not be the mechanism that separates the couple.

Preserve player inventory and companion custody. A companion can remain nearby where space permits, with normal return handling. It is not taken as an additional price for the ending.

Use a private story instance. Friends can enter a neighboring hall and rejoin the reader afterward. A second player's jokes or decisions should not speak for the reader's romantic interest.

Essential dialogue remains readable and reviewable under the mod's conversation presentation. Do not race through the tender exchanges because an ambient activity timer has expired.

First build and verification

The first useful slice is the workbench, tea, burnt toast, and Rowan asking for the reader's company after the task ends.

Write and stage that before building the disappearance. It needs to be enjoyable on its own.

Then add the disagreement, the roof, reciprocal interest, and the ordinary return indoors. Only then implement the future plan and cut.

Verify:

A quiet reader receives substantial company without automatic romantic consent.

A direct flirt receives reciprocal initiative.

A reader can disagree about the radio and repair the moment.

Every remembered act actually occurred.

The romantic and companionship accounts differ correctly.

The cut works from every ordinary actor position.

Staying close, holding hands, or walking first does not produce an accidental rescue or a blamed failure.

Missing the final glimpse does not block completion.

Reconnect preserves the relationship before the cut and the absence afterward.

The cup and breakfast scrap have one consistent identity and are never duplicated.

Leaving early invents no unplayed intimacy.

Player and companion custody survive every transition.

The final design target is specific: the player remembers someone they enjoyed being with, something that happened between them, and the small next thing they had both expected to do.
