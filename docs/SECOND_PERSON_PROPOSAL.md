# Second Person: a proposal

Design proposal, 2 November 2026. Not built. Written for the owner's review on the request: "design an intensive choose your
own adventure vignette that leans heavily on the personhood vignettes while mixing melancholic human drama and horror."

The owner asked for this draft to be written before their own proposal, *A Place for You*, was reviewed, so that neither
would borrow from the other. Their proposal arrived in full while this draft was being written. It was set aside: this draft
takes nothing from it on purpose, and the two had not been compared when this was committed. Having had it in view, I can't
promise no influence at all; any resemblance in structure (an ending set, a decision table) is the shared shape of the form.

## The idea in one paragraph

In the quiet rooms the House watched what the reader's hands did, and it has made somebody out of what it saw. It has given
them a flat above a closed launderette and one year to live in it. The reader is in the flat with them, and they cannot see
the reader. They have the reader's face as the House drew it, the comb the reader picked up and the coat the reader chose.
They have a phone that rings with the reader's kind of home on the other end. Over four seasons the reader watches the
House's guess at their life being lived, and can only do what ghosts do: move things, open doors, turn on lights, make a room
cold, or be seen in glass. Every choice is one of those acts. By spring the House has a question: it has a year here going
spare, and it would like to know whose it is.

The melancholy is an ordinary year of someone's life: moving in, a parent on the phone, someone to like, a neighbour who
dies, the question of staying. The horror is that the reader is the haunting in it. The person haunted has the reader's face,
and starts to carry the reader's habits. And when the reader looks away, the House goes on haunting in the reader's name.

## Why this leans on the personhood rooms

The resident *is* the read. Each trait is not a garnish on another story; it decides what the resident's year is about:

| Room (trait) | What it decides in the flat |
|---|---|
| Waiting room (AGE) | Why they have come to live alone here: a first flat, a fresh start after a long relationship ended, or a smaller place after a long life somewhere else. |
| Porch (HOME) | Who rings: a parent who is kind and starting to forget; someone from before whom they are hiding from; or the family they left without a word. This is the year's spine. |
| Shelter (DRAWN_TO) | Who they come to care for this autumn, and whether it is love or friendship. |
| Coatroom (SELF) | The coat by the door, how their neighbour speaks to them, and how mirrors behave. A resident read as unseen keeps the glass covered, so the reader must be seen another way. |
| Bathroom (HAIR) | The routine they keep, with the actual tool the reader chose, and the care scene in winter when they stop keeping it. |
| Kitchen table (TONE) | The drawing the reader made of their own face, copied faithfully. It is in a box in chapter 1, and in winter the resident draws it again, in the dark. |

The quiet rooms are also where the horror comes from. In winter the resident begins, in their sleep, to do what the reader did
there, in the order the reader did it.

## The rules of being unseen

The reader is an ordinary player in a private copy of the flat. Nobody in it ever answers the reader, looks at the reader or
steps aside for them. What the reader can do is plain Minecraft, read by the flat as haunting:

- **Doors.** Open and shut them. The resident notices doors that are not as they left them.
- **Lights.** Lamps and switches.
- **Things.** A handful of the resident's things can be picked up and set down somewhere else, one at a time: the routine
  object, the coat, the drawing, letters, keys, a cup, the phone's receiver. Wherever the reader puts something is where it
  lives from then on.
- **Cold.** Standing still beside the resident for a few seconds makes them shiver, pull a sleeve down and look round. They
  will not walk through the reader: they stop in the doorway the reader is standing in.
- **Glass.** Breath on a cold or steamed pane writes one word. The glass offers the words the season allows (for example
  STAY, GO, SORRY, HELLO, DON'T, ME), and choosing which is a decision.
- **Touch.** A hand on the resident is a cold hand. They flinch. What it means to them depends on the year so far.
- **Knocking.** On the thin wall to the neighbour's flat, or on a door.
- **The phone.** Lift it while it rings and hear who it is. The reader cannot speak, so the caller talks into the silence.
  It can also be left ringing, or left off the hook.
- **Being seen.** Never directly. The resident sees the reader only in reflections: the bathroom mirror, the hall glass, a
  dark window at night, the television's black screen, and a photograph the resident takes in autumn. The reader chooses
  whether to stand where a reflection falls.
- **The cupboard.** The airing cupboard by the bathroom is always warm, and the tank ticks. Going in and shutting the door
  lets the season turn. That is how the reader moves through the year, and it is always the reader's choice.

Nothing hurts the reader. There is no combat and no fail state. The vignette can be left at any time by the flat's front door
and is resumed where it was left.

### The book on the shelf

On the sitting-room shelf is a battered children's adventure book that the resident has had since they were small. Its pages
are numbered sections in the second person, and it keeps up with the flat. Its latest page describes where things stand and
lists, by number, what the reader can do now ("If you lift the receiver, turn to 31"). Doing one is turning to that page. The
reader never has to guess at the choices, and the book is the reader's record of the year. In winter the book starts to hold
pages the reader did not turn (see "When you are not looking"). At the end, it is the keepsake.

The book is the House's own voice, so it never genders the reader and never names a colour or an age.

## The flat

A first-floor flat above a launderette, closed for years, on a terraced street seen from the windows:
- a lamp post and a bus stop where nobody waits;
- a hall with a post mat, coat hooks and the hall glass;
- a sitting room with an armchair, a sofa, the phone, the shelf and the street window;
- a narrow kitchen with a table for two, a fridge and a kettle;
- a bathroom with a mirror cabinet;
- a bedroom;
- the airing cupboard;
- the landing outside, with the neighbour's door opposite.

It is built whole, at the scale of the House's other domestic places. Each season redresses it while the reader is in the
cupboard, so it changes only when nobody is looking, the House's usual rule. Through the window the street changes from late
summer to autumn rain, winter dark and a thin spring.

## The people

- **The resident.** The House's guess at the reader, wearing the reader's House body. In the House's writing they are only
  ever "they". Nobody in the flat says their name aloud. It is mumbled on the phone, lost under the line, and written on
  envelopes as the reader's own name, which is the one fact the House is sure of. They are funny when alone, untidy, and
  braver on the phone than in person. They talk to themselves, which is how the reader learns most of what they think.
- **Iris Dunne,** across the landing. Widowed, once a seamstress, still talks to her husband's chair. She brings round the end
  of whatever she cooked. She is the only one who senses the reader: "This flat's never been empty in my time. You're all
  right. Just don't frighten them." She dies in winter.
- **The person on the phone,** from HOME (see chapter 1).
- **The person they come to care for,** from DRAWN_TO (see chapter 2).
- **The letting agent** in spring, briefly, with a clipboard and a form.

All of them are authored people. No other player's observations are used for anyone.

## The year

Four chapters, one visit, about an hour for a first reading (forty minutes for a decisive one). Each chapter ends when the
reader chooses the cupboard, and the reader may stay in a season as long as they like.

### 1. Late summer: moving in

The resident comes up the stairs with the last box, puts it down and says "Right." Then, quieter: "Right. That's everything."
They sit on the box and look at the room. The book is on top of another box: "You are standing in a hall that isn't yours..."

**Why they are here (AGE).**
- **First flat** (settled DIGITAL): a laptop on a box, an air mattress until the bed comes, a parent's handwriting on the
  kitchen boxes.
- **After a separation** (settled TRANSITIONAL): boxes labelled in someone else's hand ("KITCHEN, not the good knives"), half
  of a shared life, one of each thing.
- **After a long life elsewhere** (settled ANALOG): a house sold, a chair that will not go through the door and has to stay on
  the landing, photographs with nowhere to go.
- **Tentative or unread:** a move for work, with nothing presumed. A tentative read changes only the objects.

**Decisions.**
1. **Unpacking.** The resident unpacks through the afternoon and leaves some boxes for tomorrow. The reader can put things
   away: the routine object (HAIR), the coat on its hook (SELF), the drawing (TONE) and the photograph from home (HOME). They
   can hide one instead, or leave the resident to it. Things put away are found later: "I didn't put that there." A pause:
   "I must have." A hidden thing is hunted for, then given up on, and it comes back in winter.
2. **The hall glass.** The resident hangs it. The reader can turn it to the wall or leave it facing the room. A resident read
   as unseen hangs it facing the wall themselves: "Not yet." Glass that faces the room is where the reader can be seen, and
   turning it away protects the resident from the reader, and from ever seeing them.
3. **The first night.** The resident cannot sleep. The reader can:
   - stand in the bedroom doorway ("...hello?");
   - run the kitchen tap (they get up and find it running);
   - sit in the armchair (nothing happens, which is its own answer);
   - shut the bedroom door on them. That is a kindness, and they sleep.
4. **The first call (HOME).** The phone rings while the resident is in the bath.
   - **A parent who is kind** (settled HAVEN): ringing to see they got in all right, warm, saying "Did you say Tuesday?" twice.
     Lifted, the parent talks to the silence fondly, thinking the line is bad. Left, the resident runs out dripping and
     catches it. Taken off the hook, the parent cannot get through, and is worried next season.
   - **Someone from before** (settled DANGER): a number the resident does not answer. It rings out, then rings again, and
     they turn the phone face down. Lifted, a voice says the resident's name and "I know you're there". The reader can put
     it down, or leave it off the hook all night. That protects the resident, and it is also the first thing done to them
     without asking.
   - **The family they left** (settled RUNAWAY): nobody should have this number. It rings once. The resident freezes, and it
     is a wrong number. They laugh too loudly.
   - **Tentative or unread:** an old friend.

### 2. Autumn: someone

The book opens the season with what the reader missed: "While you were in the cupboard..." The resident has a job (the shift
rota is on the fridge), a rug, and the radio left on for company. Iris knocks most evenings.

**Someone to care for (DRAWN_TO).**
- **MEN or WOMEN** (settled): a man or a woman from work, Dev or Hannah, coming round for supper for the first time.
- **BOTH** (settled): both, on different evenings, and the resident cannot decide. The reader sees both evenings and can
  influence either, but the decision is the resident's.
- **NO_ONE** (settled): Marcus, a friend who needs somewhere to live. The evening is about whether to share the flat. It
  gets as much writing and as much at stake as the others.
- **Tentative or unread:** Sam from work, with what they are to each other left for the resident to decide in the scene.

**Decisions.**
5. **Getting ready (HAIR).** The resident does their routine before the guest comes, with the tool the reader chose in the
   bathroom room, and cannot find it if it was hidden in chapter 1. The reader can set it out where they will see it. They
   say "Thanks" to nobody, then stop.
6. **The evening.** The reader can:
   - help: straighten the cloth, light the lamp, warm the room;
   - interfere: cold, a door, a window, a knock;
   - be seen: the dark window behind the resident's chair, where the guest sees someone standing;
   - leave them alone: go into the bedroom and shut the door. That is private, and it costs the reader the scene.

   The guest reacts to what happens. A knock is a joke ("Your flat's haunted"). A figure in the window ends the evening.
7. **The photograph.** The resident photographs the room to send to someone. The reader decides whether to be in the frame.
   If they are, the resident looks at the picture for a long time, then deletes it, or keeps it, depending on the evening.
8. **The second call (HOME).**
   - **HAVEN:** the parent asks the new address twice and is embarrassed.
   - **DANGER:** a letter comes with no stamp: it was delivered by hand. The reader can hide it, leave it, or put it where the
     resident will find it with the chain already on the door.
   - **RUNAWAY:** letters from a sister, Lou, forwarded and unopened, go into a drawer. The reader can open one and leave it out.
9. **The end of the evening.** The guest asks to stay, or Marcus asks to move in. The reader can make the flat cold, make it
   warm, or do nothing. The resident decides.

### 3. Winter: the loss

"While you were in the cupboard: Iris died. They went to the funeral in a borrowed coat. You were not there." The resident
comes in with a sewing tin. Iris left it to them with a note: *For you, and for your lodger.* The resident turns the note over.
"What lodger?"

This is the season the reader is seen, and the season the House begins to edit.

**The sleepwalk.** Some nights the resident gets up asleep and does, in the flat, what the reader did in the quiet rooms, in
the order the reader did it:
- sits on the hall chair as if waiting to be called;
- does the routine twice in the dark;
- puts on the coat the reader chose;
- stands at the black window as if waiting out rain;
- goes to the front door and holds the latch. HAVEN opens it to let someone in, DANGER checks the lock twice, and RUNAWAY
  walks out onto the landing towards the stairs.

  Whether to bring them back is a decision: the landing light, a sound, or a hand.
- draws a face at the kitchen table without a light on. It is the reader's drawing from the kitchen room, exactly.

The reader's own habits in the flat join the sleepwalk. If the reader always turned the lamp on at dusk, the resident starts
turning it on at dusk, without knowing why. The more the reader has done, the more of the reader they carry.

**Decisions.**
10. **The care scene (HAIR).** In grief the resident stops keeping their routine. The reader can lay the object out each
    morning. Over three mornings they go from ignoring it, to using it and crying, to using it and saying "All right. All
    right." Or the reader can leave it be.
11. **The winter beat (HOME).**
    - **HAVEN:** the parent goes into hospital. A train ticket is on the hall table. The reader can put it on the mat where it
      cannot be missed, hide it, or leave it. If the resident goes, they come back changed. If they do not, the phone rings
      at the season's end and they do not answer.
    - **DANGER:** someone is knocking at night and calling the resident's name through the letterbox. The resident is on the
      bathroom floor with the light off. The reader has the door and can:
      - leave it shut;
      - put the chain on;
      - make the hall glass show the visitor someone standing behind the door, so the visitor goes;
      - open it, in which case the visitor stands in the empty doorway, calls into the flat and leaves a note.

      Nobody is hurt and nothing violent is shown. It is about who the resident has to protect them, and whether that is
      frightening too.
    - **RUNAWAY:** Lou is on the landing, talking through the door. The reader can open it (a reunion the resident did not
      choose), leave it, or bring in the note Lou pushes under it.
12. **Being seen.** Once this winter the resident sees the reader. It happens in the bathroom mirror if it faces the room,
    otherwise in the black window, otherwise in the television. They see a person with their own face. "...That's my face."
    The reader can:
    - stay still: they stare, then ask "Are you me?";
    - step out of the reflection: gone;
    - touch them: what it means depends on everything so far;
    - write a word on the glass.
13. **Iris's tin.** Inside are needles, buttons and a half-finished sleeve. The reader can put the sleeve on the armchair
    arm, where the resident will find it and finish it, or leave the tin shut.

### When you are not looking

From winter on, the book holds pages the reader did not turn. Going into the cupboard is no longer only waiting. While the
reader is in there, the House carries on in the reader's name, and the next season opens with what "you" did:

> 74. You stood at the end of their bed all night. They lay very still so you would think they were asleep. Turn to 80.

The resident speaks of it as the reader's doing. "You were there all night." The House's version follows the reader's own
pattern: gentle if the reader has been gentle, and a little worse than the reader if the reader has been frightening. The
year contains things the reader never did and has to live alongside. It is also the only way the resident and the reader
ever share a night.

### 4. Spring: the let

"While you were in the cupboard: the tenancy came up." The letting agent comes with a clipboard. The resident can renew or go,
and is deciding:
- **stay** and make the flat theirs;
- **go** with the person they care for, or with Marcus to a bigger place;
- **go home** (HAVEN);
- **go somewhere safe** (DANGER);
- **write back** (RUNAWAY).

What they decide follows the year: who is in their life, what happened at the door, whether they finished the sleeve, and how
they feel about the flat's other occupant. Their decision is never the reader's to make.

Then the post comes. On the mat is an application for the flat, *for when the current tenant leaves*. It is filled in, in the
reader's handwriting, with the reader's name.

> 106. They will go out of that door in the morning, or they will not. Either way the flat will be let again. Somebody has
> applied for it. The form is on the mat. It has your name on it.
> If you sign it, turn to 107.
> There is no 108.

**The four endings.** Every ending is open to every profile; what came before decides each one's version.

**E1: The door on the latch.** The reader leaves by the front door, which the resident has left on the latch for them
whether they know it or not. First the reader may do one last thing:
- lay the routine object out;
- turn the glass round;
- write a word.

The resident's own decision stands, and the reader sees how it plays out from the landing window. The account is of a year
watched over.

**E2: Their name on the post.** The reader signs. For the last day of the year the reader is the one who lives there: the
person they care for knocks and greets the reader by the resident's name, and the phone rings for the reader. In the black
window, behind the reader, the resident stands where the reader used to stand, and the reader cannot make them hear.

The vignette still lets the reader go by the front door; the swap is the account's, not the reader's real self. The account
says the year was ordinary, and the reader had it.

**E3: Nobody home.** If the reader frightens the resident enough, they leave in the night with one bag and the rest of their
things where they stand. The book's last page: "They've gone. The flat is yours. Nobody can see you." The reader can walk the
flat for as long as they like. Nothing moves unless the reader moves it.

**E4: Two cups.** If, by spring, the reader has let themselves be known gently, the resident sets out two cups. They talk to
the reader through the last evening of the year: about Iris, about the phone, about the person they care for, about the face
in the glass. They ask only one thing back, and the glass offers the reader one word to answer with. Whether they stay or go,
they say "You can come," or "You can stay." The book's last line: "They will get older here. You won't."

**Aftermath.** Revisits show the flat as the ending left it:
- **E1:** the resident's life going on without reaction, or an empty flat with a forwarding address.
- **E2:** the resident in every dark window.
- **E3:** dust, and the routine object exactly where the reader last put it.
- **E4:** the second cup always out.

No revisit reopens the choices or grants a second source.

## Branching, not a score

The resident's state is not a meter. It is a short record of what has happened to them:
- what they have found moved, and whether they have said "Is somebody there?";
- whether they have seen the reader, and what the reader did then;
- whether they have spoken to the reader, and whether they have asked the reader to stop;
- whether they have left something out for the reader (Iris did, and the resident may after her);
- what the House did in the reader's name.

The same act reads differently by season and by history. A cold hand in grief, after a winter of laid-out mornings, is
company. The same hand after a winter of knocking is the end of them.

## The account and its literature

One ending gives one personal resolution: a single Witness source of the understanding kind. The quota stays derived from
`WitnessAccount.Story`. The account, its Witness line, the House of Leaves fact and the keepsake change together, as the
checklist requires.

**The account** is written from the record, never from the trait names. It opens with the first thing the resident found
moved, and names:
- the routine object, where the reader kept it, and whether the resident ever used it for the reader to see;
- who rang, and what the reader did with the phone;
- the person cared for, and how that evening ended;
- Iris, and the sleeve;
- the night the resident saw the reader, and what the reader did;
- what the House did in the reader's name, kept separate from what the reader did;
- the ending, and the one word the resident kept, if any.

It never says what any of this proves about the reader.

**The keepsake** is the adventure book, with only the pages the reader turned, in the order turned, and, in a different
type, the pages the House turned for them.

**Sample prose** for tone (to be written in full):
- "They found the comb on the shelf where you put it. They held it a while. 'I must have,' they said."
- "The phone rang on the Sunday after the funeral, at the usual time. It was nobody. They answered it anyway."
- "You were in the cupboard for most of December. They told you about it in March."

## Personhood: confidence, opt-out and a wrong read

- The read is taken as a snapshot on first entry. The vignette never changes the reader's saved read.
- **Settled** values change scenes (AGE's situation, HOME's caller, DRAWN_TO's person, SELF's address and glass).
  **Tentative** values change objects only. **Unknown, uncertain or switched off** traits get the written neutral version,
  which is a complete story, not a gap.
- A trait the server has switched off is not read, and its saved observations are not used either.
- No ending, route or decision needs any trait.
- The resident is never diagnosed and never explains themselves in category terms. If the read was wrong, it is the House's
  guess that is wrong, and the story is about the House guessing.

## Staging in the game

- **Instance.** A private copy of the flat per reader, using the existing scene-instance support. Friends who came in
  together each get their own year and compare their books afterwards.
- **The resident** is one saved literary actor wearing its reader's House body; the client already draws the reader's own
  body for mirrors and copies. They move by footpaths on a daily routine and pause, not reset, when the reader leaves.
- **Speech** is shown over their head and on the action bar, never in chat. The phone caller's lines are the receiver's.
  Long lines are kept in the book's latest page, so nothing depends on catching a disappearing line.
- **Reflections** use the existing mirror geometry: whether the reader stands where a pane reflects them to the resident's
  eye. Windows count only at night.
- **Seasons** turn behind the cupboard door with the existing fade. The builder places only what differs between seasons.
- **A companion** that follows the reader in is visible to the resident, since animals belong to their world. The resident
  talks to it and feeds it. Custody never changes; it leaves with the reader.
- **Assets.** Pixel-art materials for the flat (launderette tiles below, a seventies kitchen, a worn carpet) drawn by a tool
  checked in CI. Iris and the four autumn people are painted skins. A few subtitled sounds: the tank ticking, the street, the
  phone, and breath on glass.

## Persistence

Saved per reader:
- the chapter, and where everyone is;
- where each movable thing lives;
- the glass words;
- each decision, in order;
- what the House did in the reader's name;
- the HOME and DRAWN_TO arcs' states;
- the resident's record;
- the ending.

Leaving resumes; a restart never repeats a decision. A finished reader keeps the aftermath.

## Building it

1. **The neutral story first.** Write all four seasons, the four endings and the account with no read at all, and play it
   through. If the resident is not someone worth an hour with an unread profile, no variant will save it.
2. **First slice:** summer and autumn, the book, the cupboard, the unseen verbs and the HAVEN phone arc, ending on E1.
3. Then winter and spring, the House's pages, the other endings, and the HOME and DRAWN_TO variants as whole scenes.

## Verification that matters

- Each ending from an unread profile, and from profiles covering every trait at settled and tentative strength, using
  headless branch checks plus selected native runs (not the full product).
- Nobody in the flat ever reacts to the reader except through the listed verbs and reflections.
- A thing put somewhere stays there across seasons, reconnects and restarts.
- The sleepwalk replays the reader's own quiet-room acts in their own order, and only those.
- The House's pages follow the reader's pattern and are kept apart from the reader's own pages in the book and the account.
- Being seen happens once, in the first reflecting surface that faces the resident.
- Every ending lets the reader out by the front door. E2 changes nothing about the real reader.
- Opt-out: a switched-off trait changes nothing, and its saved observations are not quoted.
- The book and the account never gender the reader and never name a colour or an age.
- Actual-client views: the reader in the bathroom mirror from the resident's eye, the dark window, the cupboard fade, all
  four seasons through the street window, and the two cups.

## Open questions for the owner

1. Is "the reader as the ghost" the right fit for a story meant to lean on personhood, or should the reader be visible to
   somebody, as with Iris now, more often?
2. Should the House's pages from winter onwards be shown before the reader goes into the cupboard ("The House will carry on
   without you"), or discovered afterwards, which is crueller?
3. The DANGER arc's knocking at the door: is "nothing violent shown, a note left" the right limit?
4. E2: is it too bleak to sit beside the other three, or is it the point?
