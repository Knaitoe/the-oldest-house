# Borrasca: a working town, the town's day (0.4.426)

The third part of the working town (after the buildings in 0.4.422 and the keepers and pocket money in 0.4.425). The town has people
in it who are not behind a counter, a payphone that works, and a tip line that has never opened a case.

## Who is about

| Who | Where | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Mr. and Mrs. Webb** | on stools at the Hilltop Cafe's counter | the daytime visits (one to three) |
| **Roy and Jolene Tate** | in the doctor's waiting-room chairs | the first visit, expecting |
| **Dutch and Merle**, retired deputies | on the Tipple's stools | years later (visits three and four) |

They sit: an actor placed in a stool or a chair is drawn seated on it. Each has a few lines that change on each talk.

- **The Webbs.** Their Paul is the baby on the doctor's corkboard. On the childhood visits it is their first breakfast out since he
  came. Years later he is driving and works summers out at Prescott's, like everybody's kid sooner or later.
- **The Tates.** Waiting on the doctor, any day now. By the second visit they are home with their girl. Her card is the newest on the
  corkboard ("It's a girl! Paige, June 1992"), and the receptionist says the doctor was up till all hours.
- **Dutch and Merle.** Dutch did thirty-one years with the county and never once had to draw, and notes that Prescott bought the
  department its cruisers. Merle took the report on the reader's friend: "Runaway, the Sheriff said. So that's what I wrote down." On
  the last night Merle tells the reader to go home.
- **Earl** says too much now, years later: he wore the star once too and knows what was done with the reports; every child on the
  store's board was signed off as a runaway; somebody plows the road behind the depot fence. On the last night he can tell where the
  reader is going.

## The payphone

The payphone on the wall of Ridge Service opens its own dial. It has a card under the dial with the Sheriff's office, the doctor's
and 911, a window for the number, twelve keys (or the keyboard) and a hook. Seven digits, or 911, place the call. The rings come,
then what is said, a line at a time.

A call costs a quarter on the childhood visits and two quarters years later, from the purse; 911 is free, and a number not in service
gives the coin back. Prescott's number is on his truck's sign in the lot, and the Kayla poster on the last night gives the Sheriff's.

| Number | Childhood | Years later | The last night |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sheriff's office, 555-0117 | "This line's for grown-ups." Once the friend is gone: "There's no open case under that name. Kids wander off up here." | "There's no open case under that name. There's no file at all." | The Sheriff picks up, doesn't speak, asks who this is, and hangs up. |
| Dr. Hale, 555-0162 | The receptionist; the doctor is with a patient. | Booked through next month. | A machine: if you're expecting, the doctor can be reached day or night through the county at... and the tape runs out. |
| Prescott Ore Services, 555-0190 | Mr. Prescott isn't in. | "Who gave you this number?" | It rings a long time. Somebody picks up; a machine is running behind them. Nobody speaks. |
| 911 | Dispatch asks for a grown-up. | Dispatch will pass it to the Sheriff's office. | Dispatch will let the Sheriff know you called. He's up on the ridge tonight. |

## Friends pool their coins

A child who is short at a counter with the friend beside them has her put in the rest from her own pocket: a dollar, on each
childhood visit, while it lasts. "Here. I've got .25. We'll split it." When readers share Borrasca (the lower rooms' rework), their
purses will pool the same way.
