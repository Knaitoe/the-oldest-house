# The cursed film — a proposal (October 30)

The owner asked to consider a vignette based on *The Ring*, "focused more on the cursed film element, rather [than] the ghost girl climbing out the tv", with Paul Tremblay's *Horror Movie* as further inspiration. This is a proposal for review, not built yet.

*Owner's notes, October 30: the proposal sounds right; include the monster's costume in the shop, and an Easter egg for the ghost girl. Both are added below.*

*Owner's suggestions, October 30, for consideration (not final decisions): the count could be seven House days; a solo reader could pass the dub to a villager; the costume could be the vignette's artifact reward. The owner also asked that every vignette have a success and a failure state, with rewards and repercussions, as the Goatman does. The design below works the suggestions in so they can be weighed; they are listed at the end with what remains open.*

## What each source brings

- **From *The Ring*:**
  - a tape that should not be watched;
  - its images, which are clues to real places and a real death;
  - a call afterwards and a count of days;
  - the one way out: copy it and show it to someone else.

  Nothing climbs out of the screen. The dread is the film itself.
- **From *Horror Movie*:**
  - the cursed film as a made thing, with a production history: a low-budget horror film shot decades ago and never finished;
  - its existence only as leaked fragments, a screenplay and the stories of the people who made it;
  - the narrator, who played its monster.

  The vignette borrows the idea that a film can be unfinished, and that being in it is worse than watching it.

Nothing is quoted from either work. The film, its script and every line are original, as Movie Night's *Teeth of Summer* was. Neither title is used in the game.

## The vignette: "Work print" (working title)

**The place.** A small editing suite and screening room behind a closed rental shop, after hours. It has:
- the shop floor with its returns slot, and the film's monster costume on a mannequin by the counter;
- a back room with a CRT and two video decks;
- an edit bay with reels, a splicer, a light table and a wall of continuity photographs;
- a sound booth.

It is built whole, with custom textures, as the Last Stop and the Stanley were.

**The tape.** In the returns slot is an unlabelled tape. The rental card records the borrower in the reader's own handwriting, as the journal parcel and the self-addressed envelope already are.

**Watching.**
- **Playing it.** The reader puts the tape in the deck themselves, with real blocks.
- **The film.** About ninety seconds of an original film, composed shot by shot on the CRT. It is drawn in code with Movie Night's film machinery.
  - The tracking wavers.
  - The shots are deliberately ordinary and wrong: a hallway that is one of the House's own, a door seen from the wrong side, a figure at the far end of a corridor who is closer in each shot, a hand writing on a page, a room the reader has already been in.
- **Private.** Each reader's viewing is their own. A reader who looks away has not watched it.
- **The call.** When the tape ends, the shop's phone rings. It uses the phone screen the mod already has.
  - There is no voice, only a count: *seven*.
  - The count runs down over seven House days (the Overworld's days, as the journal counts them), starting from the reader's first viewing.

**The film learns them.** This is Tremblay's idea, made playable.
- **The House has been filming.** The mod already records real camera exposures and footage, and the House already keeps the places a reader has been.
- **What changes.** On each later viewing, one shot becomes the reader's own: a hall they walked, from behind, in the same grain. The figure is closer each time.
- **The cue.** By the last viewings, the reader is the figure.

**Finding out what the film was.**
- **The evidence:** the edit bay's script pages, the call sheet, the continuity photographs and the reels.
- **The story.** It tells, in fragments, of a film shot in a house that kept getting longer, by a crew who did not finish it. It ends on a last scene that was written and never shot. Nothing explains the curse outright.

**The monster's costume.** This is the owner's note, and Tremblay's idea of the person who played the monster.
- **In the shop.** The costume worn by whoever played the film's monster stands on a mannequin by the counter, with a rental tag: "NOT FOR HIRE". The tag carries the actor's name and the dates of the shoot.
- **The design is original:** a long, too-thin suit of stitched sacking, gloves with fingers a joint too long, and a hood with no face cut into it. It owes nothing to either work's monster.
- **Wearing it.** A reader can take it down and wear it. It is owner-bound, like the House's other keepsakes, and it is a whole costume over the reader's own skin.
- **What it does.** It is how the unshot last scene gets its monster (ending 2 below):
  - in co-op, one reader wears it while another films;
  - alone, the reader wears it in front of the House's camera on its tripod.
- **The footage.** While it is worn on the set, the reader-shots on the tape show the monster, not the reader.
- **Before ending 2**, it is only a costume, and the shop is a little quieter while someone has it on.

**The ghost girl, as an Easter egg.** This is the owner's note. She does not climb out; this is as close as she comes, for readers who look.
- **The well frame.** In the tape's snow after a viewing, one frame in a long while shows a stone well in a clearing. Something with long wet hair stands at its rim, far off.
- **The handprints.** A reader who stays and watches the snow after the seventh call sees wet palm prints form on the inside of the CRT's glass, then fade. Nothing comes through.
- **The returns slot.** Once, a second tape lies in it, unlabelled except for a ring-shaped water stain.
- **No reference by name.** She is never named and never drawn closer than that. None of it counts toward anything.

**Success and failure.** The film has a binary, as the Goatman's night does. Getting the count to stop is success; letting it run out is failure. Each has something to show for it.

**Success: stop the count.** There are two ways, each a personal resolution of a survival story. Either way the reader keeps the monster's costume as the vignette's artifact.
1. **Dub it.** Copy the tape on the dual deck and pass the copy on. The count stops for the reader, and the account says what that cost and who has it now. *(passed_it_on)*
   - **In co-op:** hand the copy to another reader. It is passed on only when that reader puts it in a deck and watches it themselves; nobody can be given the count without watching. Their count starts then.
   - **Alone:** carry the copy out into the Overworld and give it to a villager. The villager stands and watches nothing for a while. Seven days later it is gone from its bed, and the tape lies there instead. The account says that the reader gave it to someone who could not say no.
2. **Finish the film.** Shoot the unshot last scene on the edit bay's set with the House's camera, using the existing filming verbs. *(finished_the_film)*
   - The monster is played in the costume from the shop: in co-op, one reader wears it and another films; alone, the reader wears it in front of the camera on its tripod.
   - Splice the scene onto the reel and run the whole film once. It ends, the count stops, and the reader is in the credits.

**The artifact: the monster's costume.**
- **Taking it.** On either success the costume is the reader's own: the rental tag's "NOT FOR HIRE" is crossed out, and the reader's name is written under it in their own hand.
- **What it is.** It is owner-bound like the House's other artifacts (the tally counter, the Bone Ring), wearable anywhere, and can be offered to the Mother as a vignette artifact.
- **What it does.** A suggested effect: while the reader stands still in it, nothing in the House that hunts by sight takes them for prey. It is a small thing, in the spirit of the tally counter. The exact effect is the owner's call.

**Failure: the count runs out.**
- **What happens.** On the seventh House day, wherever the reader is in the House, they are taken into the film. They wake in the shot of the hallway, with the grain and the tracking lines over their own sight, and walk out of it into the House, alive. The House's existing rules on death and custody are unchanged. No Witness credit is given.
- **What follows them.** As the Goatman comes home with a reader who let it in, the film follows this one. It is private and never harms them:
  - now and then a screen elsewhere in the House shows the reader's own footage for a moment;
  - the grain and tracking lines flicker over their sight once in a while;
  - a phone rings, and no one speaks.
- **How it ends.** Only by coming back to the shop and stopping the count properly, by dubbing it or finishing the film. That success clears it and gives the costume.

**What it is not.**
- No ghost from the television (the Easter egg above is as close as she comes).
- No jump scare at the screen.
- The tape cannot be destroyed: pulled out and broken, it is back in the returns slot on the next arrival.

## Fit with the House

- **Witness.** It is one new eligible source, a survival story like the Goatman's: either success resolves it once, and failure resolves nothing. The pool grows to 56 sources, and the quota stays 42 (75% rounded up). Still two kinds and three endings.
- **Multiplayer.**
  - Viewings, the count and the reader-shots are private.
  - The dub is the one deliberately shared thing: a copy really passes from one reader to another, only when the other reader plays it themselves, as the owner's co-op rules for shared scenes already allow.
- **The journal.** A rubbing of the rental card, and a co-author trigger at the seventh call, are natural later links. They are not required.
- **Writing rules.** The House's writing rules apply to every line: it never genders the reader and never names a colour or an age.

## Owner's suggestions (October 30), for consideration

These are suggestions, not settled decisions. The design above follows them so they can be judged in place.
1. **Setting:** the rental shop and edit suite (Claude's proposal; not objected to).
2. **The count:** seven House days, rather than seven arrivals.
3. **Letting it run out:** the reader is taken into the film and walks out alive (Claude's proposal; not objected to). Here it is the failure state, and the film follows them until they stop the count properly.
4. **The dub alone:** perhaps a solo reader could pass it to a villager.
5. **The reader in the footage:** the House shows the reader in their own past halls (Claude's proposal; not objected to).
6. **The costume:** perhaps the vignette's artifact reward, the reader's own on either success.
7. **Every vignette has a success and a failure state**, with a reward and a repercussion, as the Goatman does. This one is a standing request, being applied across the vignettes.

Open: all of the above for the owner's final word, the costume's exact effect, and the shop's place among the halls.
