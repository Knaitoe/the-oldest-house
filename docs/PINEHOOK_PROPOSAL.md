Last Weekend at Pinehook

A separate multiplayer cabin-in-the-woods ensemble concept

Original design proposal, 2 November 2026. Not implemented.

This is a third independent story. It is not a revision of either memory vignette, A Place for You, The Ones Already Inside, or A Little Longer.

Last weekend at pinehook

Target: 2–6 players. A classic adult cabin weekend with recognizable horror archetypes, escalating danger, an actual mystery, and branching character outcomes.

The fun comes from inhabiting a trope, then having opportunities to surprise the group with it.

The central situation

Casey has invited the party and a few acquaintances to a cabin weekend. He has also arranged a fake slasher prank with Jude, an actor and friend.

The cabin owner, Len, offered Casey the place cheaply on the condition that Nina came along. Casey thinks Len wants to see her. He is too absorbed in the planned scares to ask why.

Len is blackmailing Nina over a violent robbery in her past. She knows why she has been invited. When she learns about Casey’s prank, she sees a way to kill Len and leave the apparent killer’s mask with Jude.

The party begins with a social situation and a fake threat. It becomes a real attack with someone actively trying to control what the survivors believe.

No secret cult or supernatural role machine is required. The first version is human slasher horror inside an authored House setting.

The ensemble roles

Offer the roles openly at arrival. A player chooses a part to play; the game does not diagnose their personality from a previous action.

A role supplies an arrival item, scene opportunities, optional replies, and an expectation that can be overturned. It does not dictate decisions or guarantee survival.

|Role               |The expected behavior                     |A useful reversal                                                            |An arrival object            |
|-------------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
|The Athlete        |Go first, show off, try force             |Hold a position so someone else can act; admit fear without leaving          |A heavy cooler               |
|The Skeptic        |Explain away the scare                    |Correctly expose the prank, then recognize what it fails to explain          |A camera and practical torch |
|The Charmer        |Flirt, deflect, keep the party comfortable|Notice an uncomfortable interaction and ask the question everyone avoids     |Drinks and music             |
|The Wildcard       |Be distracted, take a joke too far, wander|Notice an apparently trivial detail because they were watching something else|A battered portable player   |
|The Horror Fan     |Predict the genre and issue warnings      |Learn that a convincing horror explanation can still be wrong about a person |A terrible rented horror film|
|The Responsible One|Organize supplies, check on others        |Become angry, take an informed risk, or let someone else lead                |A first-aid bag              |

The Responsible One occupies the familiar final-girl archetype, but any player can choose it and any player can become the eventual survivor. Role labels never override forms of address or presentation.

Roles can repeat if the party prefers. Two skeptics can disagree about evidence; two athletes can respond differently to danger.

No puzzle needs all six. Important tools remain obtainable from the cabin or NPCs if their starting role is absent.

Do not make the charmer die for flirting or the wildcard receive only joke dialogue. Every role gets a moment of competence and a tempting mistake.

The people

Casey wants the weekend remembered as something he organized brilliantly. He likes these people, but has been willing to scare and embarrass them for the performance.

When somebody is hurt, he initially conceals the prank because admitting it would make the injury partly his responsibility. That concealment gives Nina room to act.

He can tell the truth early, continue directing people toward fake explanations, or help expose Nina after realizing what she is doing.

Jude agreed to wear the mask and perform two harmless attacks. He thinks Casey has told the cabin owner.

He is not prepared for real injury. If attacked, he becomes a living witness whose survival matters.

He can identify details of the second costume, explain the planned scares, and admit that he saw Nina near the boathouse. He should not automatically know every part of her motive.

Nina wants Len dead and the blackmail evidence recovered. Her response to interference is practical: change the story, recover a weapon, isolate a witness, or get the group to restrain the wrong person.

She can abandon the murder if the evidence is destroyed or exposed in a way that removes Len’s leverage. That is an authored negotiation with risks, not a single charm check.

Len is exploiting Nina’s past. He has evidence in the boathouse safe and expects her to buy it back.

He is frightened when the threat becomes real. He may ask the party for protection without admitting why Nina wants him dead.

Saving him does not require declaring him innocent. The group can protect him while exposing what he has done.

Opening: let the party be a party

Spend the first 8–12 minutes establishing the cabin and the group’s roles through activities.

Let the players choose music, settle an argument about the film, take supplies to the kitchen, inspect the dock, or start food. NPCs participate and have opinions.

Casey asks the athlete for help moving the cooler. The charmer can notice Nina becoming quiet when Len arrives. The wildcard may find a second length of mask elastic near a discarded bag. The skeptic can see a harmless prop blade before an attack occurs.

These are opportunities, not exclusive role locks. Another observant player can find the same evidence.

The horror fan can correctly predict that the power will fail. Being right about the setup does not identify who will use it.

Players establish relationships by what they actually do: who sits with Nina, who mocks Jude before knowing his part, who takes Len seriously, and who agrees to keep Casey’s surprise secret.

The first scare

Jude appears outside a window wearing the planned mask. Casey creates a distraction. A staged chase or knock follows.

The party can flee, laugh, confront him, expose the prop, or keep the game going.

Allow the skeptic to be right. An early unmasking changes the evening into an awkward confrontation with Casey rather than forcing the prank to continue unchanged.

If the party plays along, the fake scare ends with Jude withdrawing to the shed to change.

That is when Nina acts.

She tries to attack Jude, take the costume, and move toward Len. If someone accompanied Jude or kept the shed in view, they can interrupt her.

A successful interruption remains successful. Nina must improvise or abandon her plan. Do not manufacture Jude’s wound behind the player’s back to preserve a cutscene.

The turn

In the ordinary route, Jude returns injured and without the mask.

He sees Casey.

> Jude: Who else did you hire?
>
> Casey: Nobody.
>
> Jude: Then tell me who was in the shed.

At the same time, a masked figure crosses the far end of the dock.

The player who uncovered the original prank now has a reason to admit a second problem. The player who dismissed the wildcard’s small observation may need to listen.

The group has a story to investigate and a wounded person to help.

If Jude’s injury was prevented, the equivalent scene confronts Casey and Nina with an actual witnessed attack. It can reach the crisis faster, with more people alive and fewer uncertainties.

Parallel scenes

The party can divide voluntarily among several tasks.

The kitchen: keep Jude alive and talking.

A player can provide first aid while another checks the costume plan with Casey. Jude’s account is interrupted by pain and by Casey trying to minimize what happened.

The responsible player can insist that Casey stop talking over him. Another role can do exactly the same work.

The dock: recover what happened.

Blood, costume ties, a practical escape route, and a moved boat establish the attack’s sequence. A camera can preserve evidence but is never an exact-angle completion check.

The wildcard’s earlier object can contradict Nina’s statement about never going outside.

The boathouse: protect or confront Len.

Len wants a locked door between himself and the attacker. He also wants nobody opening the safe.

The group can keep him alive, demand the evidence, bargain with Nina through the window, or trust Len and help corner her.

The cabin: expose the prank.

Casey’s plan lists harmless effects and locations. Those details separate a staged noise from a real approach. Let the party use this knowledge.

Destroying the plan in embarrassment is a consequential act. It removes a convenient explanation of the props and can make Jude easier to frame.

None of these routes is a mandatory clue token. Early evidence or a direct confession can move the story forward sooner.

Puzzles with consequences

Power and light. The circuit can illuminate the dock or the cabin’s rear approach fully, but a damaged junction cannot initially sustain both. Repairing it takes exposure and time. The group can choose which person to protect while another works.

The costume timeline. Compare what Jude wore, where his props were placed, and the extra mask fastening. This exposes a second use of the costume. No arbitrary numeric code.

The boathouse door. An old brace and jammed bolt can keep Len safe or trap him with the attacker. The same mechanism has different moral uses. Another player can operate the release from the dock.

The safe. Len can open it, be persuaded to reveal the release, or be bypassed through a physical inspection puzzle. The evidence is not available only to one character class.

Emergency transport. Getting a vehicle or boat ready is one practical branch during the crisis. It can evacuate Jude, move Nina, or let Casey flee. It does not stand in for resolving the characters’ conflict.

All required tasks have fallback tools and a slower solo operation. A lost role or disconnected player cannot make them impossible.

The crisis

Bring the active plans together rather than gathering everyone for a final vote.

• Nina approaches Len if she still has a weapon and expects the evidence to remain.
• Len tries to keep the safe shut if the group has not confronted him.
• Casey may reveal the prank publicly or try to take the costume away before anyone sees it.
• Jude can identify an attacker, refuse to help Casey, or ask a player to stay while the others leave.
• A participant can carry the evidence toward Nina, Len, or the rest of the party.

These are interruptible physical actions. Who is alive, informed, armed, or confined matters.

The group can support opposing plans. A shared NPC fate follows the action that actually occurs; individual accounts preserve responsibility.

No player is secretly selected as the murderer in the cooperative version. An adversarial player role would be a separate mode with explicit expectations.

Outcome families

Expose both.

The party prevents the murder, brings the evidence into the open, and keeps Len from using it privately again.

Nina may surrender her weapon or need to be restrained. Len can survive without being vindicated. Casey must face the damage of the staged threat.

A good outcome can be earned. Do not kill a rescued person at dawn just to restore the body count.

Help Nina finish it.

The party knowingly helps her reach Len or leaves him trapped when it could intervene.

Nina retrieves or destroys the evidence. Casey and Jude can learn who enabled the killing. Nina’s gratitude does not make the survivors agree about what happened.

This is an alliance with a person and her crime, not a different exit.

Protect Len and accept his account.

The party restrains or kills Nina while allowing Len to retain the evidence and conceal his part.

Jude’s observations and surviving objects can make the final story visibly incomplete. A participant who knows more can still expose Len afterward, changing the coda.

The performance becomes the story.

If Jude dies and Casey conceals the prank, Len and Nina can each try to place the costume and blame with the dead actor.

The party can challenge that account, participate in it, or leave in disagreement. Track the actual evidence and statements. Do not declare a false public story inevitable when someone has already shown the truth.

Specific deaths, rescues, betrayals, and departures create variations within those families.

Role payoffs

The athlete can be the person who holds a brace instead of chasing the killer.

The skeptic can be responsible for both exposing the prank and persuading people to take the real attack seriously.

The charmer can break the comfortable party mood by asking Nina what Len said to her.

The wildcard can identify a small overlooked object and become the person the others finally listen to.

The horror fan can admit that a perfect genre theory pointed at the wrong person.

The responsible one can save Jude and still make a morally ugly choice about Len.

The roles supply expectations. The player’s acts supply the character arc.

Practical multiplayer requirements

One shared scene state; no host-only plot decision. Separate private conversations can occur, with explicit ways to tell another NPC what was learned.

Keep participant profiles independent and preserve their established bodies and address. A chosen archetype is a fiction role, not a new inference about age, gender, attraction, or morality.

Save tool custody, evidence ownership, completed repairs, who knows the prank, who witnessed an attack, NPC injuries, and each person’s current plan.

A disconnected actor leaves a held mechanism in a stable state. Critical evidence remains recoverable. No ending requires an exact headcount or every role.

Ordinary speech, notes, voice chat, and observation can all help. No killer victory depends on players failing to remember an out-of-band secret.

Art should remain vanilla-adjacent: a useful cabin, a plausible dock and shed, costumes that can be inspected, and human faces that remain human when frightened.

The most useful first slice is arrival, one fake attack, an early unmasking branch, and the real attempted attack on Jude. It should already demonstrate that a role can be funny, competent, and wrong in the same evening.
