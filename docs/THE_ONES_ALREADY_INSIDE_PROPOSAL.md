# The Ones Already Inside (the owner's proposal)

Recorded verbatim, 2 November 2026: the owner's design for an original multiplayer choose-your-own-adventure vignette. Not
built. The owner asked that it be started only after everything before it is finished (After the People Lights, A Place for
You, Second Person, the Fuse Board and the Header Tank). Nothing below is edited; the owner's line breaks, tables and headings

The Ones Already Inside

An original multiplayer choose-your-own-adventure vignette

Design proposal, 2 November 2026. For This House Has Ghosts in It. Not implemented.

Horror is the primary experience. Human sorrow is an aftermath of what people do under pressure. This is a new original story; A Place for You remains a separate private vignette.

Recommended first-build party size: 2–6. Target length: 45–65 minutes, with shorter routes possible. One shared instance, one physical history, and individual participation and consequences. A solo fallback can use the same story with slower, noisier mechanisms.

The narrative promise

A man called Dan has returned to the House with his friend Rae because his body is failing. Rae asks the arriving party to stay with him while she finds the housekeeper who promised to repair him.

The party discovers someone else being kept there: Daniel, Dan's living original.

Years ago, Daniel accepted a week's rest while a borrowed version of him took over outside. The week ended. Daniel never got out. Dan was told that the original had gone on with his own life.

The House has been sustaining Dan with Daniel's body.

Dan has made friendships, done things Daniel never did, and become someone Rae knows. Daniel has spent those same years conscious in a locked room. He wants his body, his name, and the rest of his life back.

Tonight the housekeeper Vera intends to finish the arrangement. One Daniel becomes permanent. The other is consumed.

The players arrive during that event. Their central task is to decide what they will help happen, and then live through the consequences of their involvement.

Finding a way out is a practical concern during the horror. It is not the story's central decision or the basis of its ending families.

The central question is concrete: What will you do when protecting one person may require destroying another, and both can ask you for help?

The supernatural facts

These facts belong to this story's house. Do not make them universal laws for every double in the mod.

The House can prepare a borrowed person from an original and a small set of observations. The preparation produces a convincing surface, not a complete human history.

A borrowed person can acquire experiences and relationships of their own. The House's intention does not settle what they subsequently become.

An unfinished body is not yet connected to its original. The party's bedroom doubles are in this state.

Dan is connected to Daniel. The link sustains Dan, drains Daniel, and transmits physical injuries between them.

Returning the borrowed body through its room's frame can end the dependency and release the original without inflicting an ordinary fatal injury on either. Dan then ceases to exist.

Completing the arrangement consumes Daniel and makes Dan independent of that source. A residue of the original may remain; Vera does not have complete control over it.

Severing the room system releases its originals but deprives borrowed bodies of their support. They do not instantly vanish. They begin to come apart.

Killing a linked body by ordinary violence also kills its counterpart. Establish this danger visibly before it can determine a main ending.

Vera knows more than anyone else, but can lie about motives and costs. Give every indispensable rule a second source: observable injury, Daniel's experience, Dan's deterioration, or a small reversible demonstration. No notebook contains the entire solution.

The house needs people physically present to finish its new arrangements. It cannot bind a player just because somebody else selected their name in conversation.

Opening Daniel's door or restraints frees his movement but does not end the link. He can physically leave the room and still continue losing his life to Dan. Getting both men through an exterior door therefore cannot substitute for deciding what to do about their connection.

The cast

Daniel: the person kept in the room

Daniel is exhausted, angry, and frightened of being dismissed as an inconvenient earlier version. His room has ordinary evidence of years passing badly: repairs made to the same chair, food pushed through a small hatch, a wall marked by repeated attempts to measure time.

He does not beg politely through every scene. He can demand help, refuse to trust Rae, or reach for a weapon.

His first clear request is practical: get him out of the frame before Vera brings Dan downstairs.

He initially thinks Dan knowingly stole his life. Learning otherwise changes his accusation, not his desire to live. If he learns about Dan's independent life, he can understand it and still refuse to surrender his own.

He does not inherit ownership of Rae's affection or of relationships Dan formed after the substitution.

Dan: the person who came back

Meet him before learning what he is. He helps hold a door, notices when another person has fallen behind, and talks to Rae in a familiar, slightly impatient way. He should be someone the group has encountered as a person.

He came back because his body is failing. A finger will not straighten. His reflection holds an expression after he has stopped making it. Rae persuaded him the House could repair him.

He did not know Daniel was still being used. When he learns, he is appalled. That does not make him ready to die.

He can offer to go back, ask the party to find another answer, or panic and try to complete the arrangement. These are responses to specific conversations and acts, not outcomes of an invisible goodness score.

His insistence that his own life happened is true within the story.

Rae: the person who brought him

Rae knows Dan through a life outside the House. They can be close friends without any romance. Their bond is authored and independent of players' attraction reads.

She knew before arriving that Daniel was alive. She concealed that from Dan because she feared he would refuse the visit.

She told herself the House could save both. Vera has privately told her something less generous.

Rae wants the players' help, but she is also trying to recruit witnesses who will understand why she chose Dan. If cornered, she can hide the release key, take it to Vera, or help Daniel after admitting what she did.

She is capable of changing course. A single gentle reply does not erase her fear.

Vera: the broker

Vera runs the arrangements. She is composed, observant, and physically involved: carrying a basin, setting a chair upright, holding a door while something heavy moves behind it.

She treats the borrowed person's continued usefulness as evidence that the arrangement should be completed. Her language is ordinary and chilling: the original has been cared for, Dan has a life to return to, surely the players can see what would be wasted.

She is herself a completed borrowed person. Her original died long ago. This can be learned, but it is not a master switch that solves the house.

She wants Dan's completion to succeed and the party's prepared rooms to become future business. If the group becomes unmanageable, she tries to preserve the system and abandon its current occupants.

She can be killed, restrained, deceived, or driven out. None of those actions automatically resolves Daniel and Dan's link.

The house

Use a compact lodging house with a front hall, a parlor, a bedroom gallery, a workroom, and service passages behind the rooms. A cellar contains the room frames' shared support.

Keep the main NPC pair's room distinguishable from the party's prepared rooms. There is one linked frame for Daniel and Dan; sleeping party copies are unfinished.

The horror

The prepared bodies

Each participant has a room containing an unfinished body in their current House appearance. One familiar routine object is placed beside it when the personhood read permits that callback.

All players can see a body and its room. A private reaction can differ, but the creature does not become a different species for each observer.

The first body opens its eyes while its original is standing nearby. It does not recognize the other players. It looks toward Vera.

Later, a body rehearses an action the player previously made: reaching for a routine object, turning a glass, hanging a coat. It continues beyond the point where that movement should stop.

These bodies demonstrate the fate Vera is preparing for the group. They are not an impostor identification game.

Failed borrowed people

Two or three failed bodies inhabit the service spaces. Their origins are suggested through worn clothes and scraps of human behavior. They have continued to function after their arrangements went wrong.

They pull at doorframes before entering, fit through spaces with movements that expose the wrong proportions beneath their clothes, and try to bring living visitors into the prepared rooms.

They are physically persistent. Closing a door delays them; they batter the latch before breaking through. They follow noise and reachable paths. They do not teleport to punish someone for knowing a rule.

Combat can stagger them and create room to act. Defeating one does not choose what happens to Daniel and Dan.

A taken player remains active in the service passages. They can make noise, work a local release, or help a rescue from the other side. This is an opportunity for cooperation, not a long spectator sentence.

The visible link

Dan cuts his hand during an early disturbance. The same cut opens on Daniel's hand elsewhere.

If the party is divided, two players can witness opposite sides. If they stay together, the second injury can be found afterward and described by Daniel. Sharing that information helps establish what violence would do.

Later, Dan loses his breath while Daniel is held against the frame. Different players may be physically present to stop the restraint and support the person collapsing.

The body horror is an event the group can affect. A text entry saying that two people are connected is insufficient.

Act I: They brought him back

Target: 8–12 minutes. Establish the people, then expose the impossible situation.

N1. Rae's request

Rae intercepts the group at the hall. Dan stands with her, concealing a hand he cannot straighten. She asks the party to stay with him while she finds Vera. Dan wants somebody present when Vera examines him.

Dan is willing to enter. He asks Rae not to leave him alone with Vera.

Players can agree, question them, speak to Vera first, or decline involvement. Rae says there was an arrangement here once and Vera promised to put it right. Dan does not yet know what Rae has learned about Daniel. An early departure is possible but leaves the story unresolved.

Different players can make different promises. Rae cannot turn one person's agreement into the whole party's commitment.

N2. Your rooms

Vera welcomes everyone and directs them toward prepared rooms. The unfinished bodies are discovered immediately, within the opening few minutes.

The party can refuse the rooms, inspect one, let Vera explain, or interrupt her preparation. No acceptance is necessary for the main story.

Dan sees a body and recognizes something he did not want to name about himself.

If a player privately asks what is wrong with him, he can admit that he has stopped sleeping because he is afraid of what his body does when he is not watching it.

N3. The living original

One or more players reach Daniel. He recognizes Dan's voice through the wall and becomes frantic.

Players can tell him Dan has returned, conceal that, promise to free him, or go back for Dan before touching the frame.

Freeing him now changes the next act. He is mobile, angry, and able to confront Rae. Leaving him confined gives Vera time and gives the group another reason to split its attention.

The player is not required to imprison him to receive the full script.

N4. The injury

The first failed body intrudes while Vera is moving between rooms. Dan helps keep it away from someone. Glass breaks; his hand is cut. Daniel suffers the same injury.

The group can drive the intruder back, protect an NPC, check Daniel, or pursue Vera's apparent failure to control it.

Do not guarantee an NPC injury by cancelling a successful intervention. If the group prevents the cut, use a smaller, deliberate and reversible demonstration later with both men's knowledge. The indispensable information survives; the players' success remains real.

The first act ends with the knowledge that neither man can be treated as a disposable imitation without consequences.

Act II: What each person wants

Target: 12–18 minutes. Substantial conversations can happen simultaneously, and actions alter who appears next.

N5. Daniel's account

He describes the promised week and what actually happened. He wants the release key.

He may ask for a weapon if frightened or betrayed. Giving him one creates a concrete danger because of the link. A player who has learned the injury rule can warn him; a player who has not can ask him to wait.

If he attacks Dan and the group does not intervene, both can die. That is a narrative consequence, not a random bad ending selected by dialogue tone.

N6. Dan's account

Dan describes something he did outside that mattered to somebody else. Use one specific incident Rae can later confirm. He is not reciting an autobiography to prove he deserves life.

Tell him Daniel is still being drained and he first denies what Vera told him, then asks to see the room.

Conceal it and he continues to seek a repair. If he learns the truth later, the confrontation includes who knew and who allowed him to proceed.

A player can promise to stand with him without promising Daniel's death. He can ask what that promise means when the crisis comes.

N7. Rae's confession

A player catches Rae withholding the release key, or asks directly about a contradiction in her story.

She admits she knew Daniel was alive. She can hand over the key, ask the player to hide the truth, or take it to Vera.

This is a turning point. It changes the scene that follows:

N8. Vera's offer

Vera offers a repair for Dan and help for the group. She proposes that the players keep Daniel calm and return him to the frame.

She initially describes this as making the arrangement stable. If challenged with evidence, she admits Daniel will not survive completion.

The cost must be known before the party can help complete it. Vera's euphemism is a character's deception; the game does not hide the consequence from a player committing the final act.

She can also show how returning Dan through the frame would free Daniel. She presents this as wasteful, not impossible.

N9. The cellar

A player following the service passages finds the frames' common support and a failed body no longer connected to its original.

A small local release demonstrates that the borrowed body begins to lose cohesion when its connection is severed. It remains conscious.

This establishes the third option: break the system so it cannot consume the originals, while accepting that the borrowed people will begin to fail.

The discovery is not an automatic good solution. It creates another conversation and another human decision.

Act III: Put them in the same room

Target: 10–15 minutes. This is a crisis shaped by earlier acts, not a fixed reunion cutscene.

N10. The confrontation

If Daniel is free, he comes to the parlor. If confined, somebody must decide whether to bring him out or bring Dan to him. If one is dead, do not respawn him to preserve this scene.

Dan sees the original's condition. Daniel sees the person who has been living outside.

Rae may defend Dan, admit what she knew, or be absent because she has gone to Vera. Vera may try to retake Daniel or persuade Dan to accept completion.

A short base exchange:

Daniel: She said a week.

Dan: She told me you were gone.

Daniel: I wasn't.

Rae: He didn't know.

Daniel: Then why did you?

Let that last question go to Rae. Players can answer, interrupt, or let her face it.

This is where distributed information becomes narrative action. A player can reveal a private confession, keep a promise they made, expose another participant's lie, or decline to speak for someone else.

The game cannot know an out-of-band voice conversation. It tracks who a player explicitly told in-world. A clear "Tell them what Rae said" option lets players translate shared information into the story without repeating an investigation.

N11. The first attempt

An NPC now acts on their current plan.

Rae, if still pursuing repair, tries to guide Dan toward Vera.

Daniel, if frightened and armed, tries to stop him by force.

Dan, if informed and supported, asks for time to choose rather than allowing Rae to decide.

Dan, if cornered or deceived, may seize Daniel and demand completion.

Vera, if exposed, uses a failed body to drive the group apart and takes control of the frame.

These are staged, interruptible actions with clear physical beginnings. Their order is determined by the established state, not by whichever NPC monologue happens to play first.

The party can split around this crisis: protect Daniel, keep Dan upright, prevent Rae taking the key away, or stop Vera's approach.

That is multiplayer cooperation and disagreement inside an unfolding event.

N12. A body is taken

During the disturbance, a failed body tries to drag a visitor or an NPC into a prepared room.

Who is exposed follows actual positions. Do not always select the least experienced player or a profile category.

Saving somebody can interrupt another plan. Leaving them to finish the frame may make a later rescue necessary. A taken player can open a service latch from inside while the rest of the party argues in the parlor.

Capture does not immediately complete a player substitution. A final player-specific bargain always requires that player's own explicit act. Ordinary creature damage and death remain possible.

Vera's attempt to use the visitors is part of the event. If the party supports completion, she brings one of their unfinished doubles to the parlor and asks its original to help steady it at the frame. If the party opposes her, she sends a failed body to obtain a visitor instead. Her proposed repair and her preparation of the party are the same activity. This makes the threat to the players an action by a character, rather than a separate chase inserted between conversations.

The crisis resolves into a choice the players can physically help carry out, a violent failure, or a temporary interruption while an independent rescue is attempted.

Act IV: What the party helped make happen

There are three deliberate resolution families and one violent failure. The shared fate of Daniel and Dan is real for everyone. Individual endings differ in participation, promises, rescue, betrayal, survival, and what each person actually witnessed.

E1. Return Daniel

Dan is returned through the frame, releasing Daniel. Dan ceases to exist as a separate person.

This can happen through Dan's informed agreement, through the party coercing him, or through Vera exploiting his panic. Those are different authored scenes and accounts.

If he refuses, a polite final reply cannot make that refusal disappear. The group must choose whether to use force or pursue another option.

Rae's response depends on what she did and what she was told. She may help, resist, or be unable to watch. She never automatically accepts Daniel as the continuation of her friend.

Daniel is free. He has lost years and now knows that another person lived them. He may thank one player and refuse to look at another.

Final image: Daniel asks Rae about a detail of Dan's outside life. She answers a player instead. He realizes that being the original does not make the memory his.

The horror has ended in an actual erasure. Do not write a triumphant rescue summary.

E2. Complete Dan

Daniel is brought to the frame and consumed. Dan becomes independent of the link.

The players must know what the completion costs. Daniel is alive, can object, and can ask a specific participant to stop.

Dan's role matters. He may take part knowingly, allow Rae and Vera to do it while he looks away, or arrive too late to stop a process another person began. Those outcomes cannot share the same account.

Completion is staged through architecture and bodies: the occupied room becomes shallower, the chair is pressed toward a wall that has no space behind it, and Daniel's voice continues after the room is gone.

Dan survives. Rae can reach him. Then a second voice comes out beneath his first sentence: Daniel, still trying to finish the request he was making.

Vera calls it a temporary effect. Nobody can verify that reassurance.

Final image: Dan holds a hand over his own mouth. Rae tries to take it away because she wants him to speak to her.

This is a horror outcome produced by a decision about people, not simply by selecting a dangerous exit.

E3. Sever the house

The party releases the common support and opens the linked frames.

Daniel and the other originals are freed. Dan and the failed borrowed people lose the support that has held their bodies together.

The release is enacted in the cellar and at the occupied frame. A ratchet permits the tasks to be performed sequentially if somebody is absent; an available partner makes the action faster and less exposed.

Both Daniels must be told what this means if the party wants their agreement. Withholding the cost is possible and remembered.

The house's inhabitants react differently. Some borrowed people beg for the connection to be restored. One attacks a freed original, trying to force itself back into place. Another sits down because it understands what has happened.

Dan can help protect Daniel even while his hands lose their shape, or panic and try to return to the frame. His established intentions and the party's treatment affect this action.

Rae can follow Dan or help Daniel. The party can make room for both without being able to promise both long lives.

Final image: the two men sit within sight of the outside threshold. One is struggling to remember how to stand without the frame. The other is holding the shape of a hand together under his sleeve. Neither can finish helping the other.

The group has stopped the process. It has not made its victims whole.

E4. Neither Daniel

Ordinary lethal violence against a linked body kills both.

Support this as a real branch if the player or an NPC takes that action after the link has been established. Do not spring it as a punishment for an innocent first experiment.

Rae may hold Dan while Daniel dies in another room. Players may be divided between the two deaths.

Vera immediately begins restoring the room. Her efficiency can be more frightening than the deaths themselves.

A subsequent choice still matters: expose her to Rae, stop her preparing another guest, leave her the house, or destroy the support. Those actions determine the aftermath, but they cannot revive either man.

Final image: Vera has prepared two places at a table for the next arrivals. Rae knocks one chair over and cannot say which place she meant to remove.

Withdrawal and personal failure

A player can leave the unfolding story while others continue. Their personal account says what they left and what they knew. It does not invent the eventual resolution.

A dead participant returns through the mod's existing death handling. A taken participant remains active until rescued, self-released, withdrawn through an available route, or killed.

The party's story can continue after one player's personal endpoint. Completion is never contingent on every entrant being simultaneously present.

A specific multiplayer branch

Three players divide their attention.

Morgan meets Daniel and promises to release him.

Jules hears Rae's confession and promises to help Dan.

Sam finds the cellar and sees what severing does.

Morgan uses the key. Daniel reaches the parlor while Jules is keeping Dan there. Rae sees Daniel and realizes Morgan has acted before she was ready.

Jules tells Dan that Rae knew. Dan turns away from her. Daniel sees the hesitation and mistakes it for a plan to abandon him again.

Sam arrives with the third option. Rae asks whether Dan would survive it. Sam can answer honestly, soften the truth, or say they do not know how long he would have.

If Jules gives Vera the key to keep Dan alive, Vera starts moving toward completion. Morgan can release Daniel from her grip while Sam blocks a failed body entering the room. Dan now has to decide whether to help Daniel or allow the completion Jules promised to support.

If Jules instead refuses Vera, Rae must decide whether to seize the key herself. If she does, a player can physically prevent her leaving; that creates a confrontation with Rae, not a different corridor puzzle.

The next scene changes according to who acted, who learned the truth, and whether Daniel is still free. There is no party leader's dialogue choice that substitutes for those events.

Personhood as an instrument of horror

Take a separate permitted snapshot for each participant on entry. It changes what the House has prepared and which temptation Vera can plausibly offer. It never determines a player's morality or assigns their side in the conflict.

Multiplayer decisions and authority

One shared NPC fate is unavoidable: Daniel cannot be alive for one player and dead for another in the same room. Different players can have different responsibility for that fate.

Ordinary actions happen when an actual participant performs them. There is no privileged host.

A promise binds the speaker's authored relationship, not every party member.

Anyone can provide a fact in a shared conversation. An NPC then knows it.

A private confession remains unknown to an NPC until somebody reveals it.

Critical mechanisms expose a visible preparation stage and a clear final action.

Another player can oppose a plan by freeing the person, taking back an object through an interaction, confronting the instigator, or operating a reversible release.

Resolve conflicting final actions in server order. Revalidate the frame and both bodies before each commit. Never execute two incompatible resolutions.

Once a person has died or been unmade, subsequent actions respond to that fact.

No vote can volunteer another player's body or alter another player's global personhood record.

The game should not try to detect the group's actual beliefs or voice chat. It tracks explicit actions, statements made to characters, and physical events.

A critical conversation happens at a defensible staging point. Threats do not advance through a required reading panel and kill someone unable to move. Other participants can still act elsewhere. Avoid globally freezing every creature because one player has left a menu open.

Authoring scope

Write a complete neutral script first. Then add personhood fragments to arrival, prepared rooms, Vera's offer, the crisis, and the personal account.

The first full story needs:

four named NPCs with changing aims;

the shared injury and its alternate demonstration;

a mobile Daniel and a confined Daniel version;

the consequences of Rae keeping or surrendering the key;

Dan knowing or not knowing Rae's deception;

a confrontation that respects absence, injury, and prior action;

three deliberate resolutions and the linked-death branch;

active taken-player service spaces;

six personal room sockets and a small failed-body cast;

saved shared facts plus per-player promises, knowledge, and participation.

Aim for roughly 18–24 authored decision points. Depth comes from their consequences, not from adding another dozen levers.

Do not write every combination as a separate script. Use a shared scene skeleton with explicit entry conditions and authored substitutions. An absent character cannot deliver a critical line; indispensable knowledge must have another delivery path.

The smallest useful prototype includes Rae's request, one prepared room, Daniel's release, the injury, Rae's confession, and the parlor confrontation. Two players must be able to trigger meaningfully different next scenes before building the final departure staging.

Verification

Verification should establish narrative continuity as well as multiplayer operation:

Two players support different men and both make consequential physical interventions.

The whole group stays together and still receives a coherent story.

The party divides every available private conversation.

A successful build lets a party tell the story of what they did to these people, why they disagreed, and which decision they wish they had interrupted. Surviving the building is one part of that account.
