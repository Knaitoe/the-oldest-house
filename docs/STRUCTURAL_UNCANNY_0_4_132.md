## The Silo — unrecovered-bodies

There is no monster. A girl escaped a bad home and hid in the rafters of an abandoned grain silo. Her father, a sheriff, spent years looking for her. An unrecovered body lets him keep inventing possible lives for her. The silo is an endless rotting climb away from the grain where she died.

Entry is on a crust of corn. Above it, a house and barn rot into each other: suspended half-collapsed kitchen tables, horizontal bedroom doors used as bridges, auger pipes, a porch swing, a shattered window frame and a rusted ladder. These are remains of a place, not a neat parkour course.

Actual weight strains the wooden bridges. Staying on one makes it groan, sag and tilt until the body falls back into the grain. Motion and jumping follow the physical failure of the wood. The corn contains the actual buried history. A muffled police radio comes from below; a father's voice rehearses lives she might have had: “She took a bus to Tucson,” “She met a boy with a flatbed,” “She's just waiting out the winter.” When a sentence finishes, the boards rot faster. No monster audio.

Three physical text pieces, in this order:

Bottom of the ladder:

> An unrecovered body is a wonderful thing for a town. So long as they don't drag the river or empty the bin, a girl is just a blank sheet of paper, and everybody in the county gets to write their favorite ending on her. She’s in California. She’s waiting tables in Omaha. She’s anywhere but twelve feet down in the dark with her mouth full of yellow dust.

Halfway up, on a kitchen table wedged between steel beams:

> He came out to the farm twice a week with his cruiser lights off, just sitting in the gravel with the window rolled down, looking up at the tin roof. I think he knew the bridge gave out under my sneakers the night I climbed up here. You can't look at a broken board for three winters and not know how weight works. He just preferred the version where I grew wings at the last second.

At the roof hatch:

> When you wish for a life where nobody ever leaves, you don't realize how heavy eternity gets when it's trapped in a round room. Go ahead and open the hatch. The sky out there is just painted tin, and the corn is still waiting at the bottom for both of us to get tired of climbing.

## The Backseat — steering-wheel-lock

The scare happens inside apparent sanctuary. It is not announced by completing chores or breaking a fourth prop.

A wet two-lane blacktop at night, towering dark pines and a warm idling station wagon. Headlights cut the rain. Wipers click. A faint original four-chord song comes from the dashboard. The driver's door has been left wide open. A notebook is on the passenger seat; its account can be read from the dashboard area before closing the door.

The player physically enters or sits in the driver's seat and closes the real door. At that click the radio dies, child locks clunk down and rain stops immediately. Keep the player's actual view in first person in the seat. The wheel and locked door provide no escape. A natural look toward the mirror or passenger seat triggers the reveal without a countdown: the figure flattened against the back floorboard sits violently upright behind the shoulder, appears in the rearview mirror and fills the space inches behind the camera. At the same moment the car lunges toward the black trees at an apparent 80 mph. Windshield glass comes inward; steel folds; the impact cuts to black mid-crash and returns that reader to the House. Preserve carried belongings and independent peers.

Dashboard account:

> Cars have a lie built right into the upholstery. You hunker down behind two tons of Detroit steel and safety glass, frost on the AC and four chords on the radio, and you think you’re in your own private vacuum. You think the world outside the windshield is just a movie you’re driving through.
>
> I only left the door open for twenty seconds while I went back to drop the house keys in the mailbox. Twenty seconds under the streetlamp with the dome light shining on the vinyl.
>
> The steering column locked up a quarter-mile past the county line, right as the pines closed in over the median. I could see the oncoming headlights swerving into my lane, and I was already apologizing to my mother in my head for the dent in the fender, right up until I looked in the rearview mirror and realized the backseat wasn't empty anymore—and whatever climbed in while I was at the mailbox was the one holding the wheel steady.

## Missing Sleep / The Split-Feed Bedroom — complicity

The boy murdered his parents in ecstatic dissociation. The recorded image is the forgiving lie he tells himself. The player inhabits his walk; there is no separate marionette to evade and no camera-management or sleep-until-dawn puzzle.

A single long, cluttered family hallway leads to the closed master-bedroom door. Mirrors and televisions show the player's reflection walking casually, hands in pockets. The player's own first-person body and wallpaper shadow show arms fixed out at ninety degrees, jerking with each actual step. Wet ecstatic purring belongs inside that reader's own audio channel. The player walks this body toward the bedroom door.

Account:

> I bought the cameras because I wanted proof that I was asleep when it happened. A man ought to be unconscious when his life ends.
>
> Look at the television in the hall and you’d almost forgive me. Look at the way I move on the tape—loose in the shoulders, stepping right over my sister’s shoes, whistling something without a tune like I’m just headed down to the kitchen for a glass of milk. That’s a boy who loves his mother. That’s a boy who doesn't know how heavy iron gets when you swing it.
>
> Don't look at the carpet. Look at the screen. If you only watch the glass, you don't have to hear that wet, clicking sound in the back of my throat, and you don't have to know how good it felt to let the strings pull me all the way to their bed.

## The Blue-Light Diner — consumption and erasure

Claire died on the bypass. What wears her uniform is an empty hungry absence. The woman at the counter protects herself by letting strangers sit in booth four. She watches silently and wipes the counter while the consumption happens. Remove helpful warnings and the plate/eating survival rules.

The reader sits with the actual menu. Each forward page turn brings the other waitress one booth closer: a big girl with a stretched, unrecognizable photograph-smile. As she approaches she eats the reader's presentation of self: health display, hotbar and finally the room's saturation disappear. Those are personal visual effects; preserving actual inventory custody is still required. A framed eight-by-ten by the register gives the smile its history. Progress follows actual reading actions, not an attack countdown.

Menu account:

> People think a ghost is a memory that got stuck in the wallpaper. They want to believe that when Claire died out on the bypass, some sweet little piece of her came back here because she missed the smell of coffee and the Friday night crowd.
>
> Ghosts aren't people. What was Claire went into the dirt, and what came through the door in her uniform is just a hole in the shape of a girl. It’s empty all the way to the bottom, and it’s always hungry.
>
> She learned how to do the smile from the framed eight-by-ten we kept by the register—the way Claire used to pull her lips tight and show all her teeth so the camera wouldn't catch how heavy she’d gotten. Only this thing pulls the corners back until the skin splits by her ears. I don't chase her out anymore. So long as a stranger pulls off the highway and sits in booth four before midnight, she eats what’s inside their head and leaves mine alone.
