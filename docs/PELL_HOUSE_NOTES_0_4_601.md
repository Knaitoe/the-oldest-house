## A ball, and catch with Callum

### The ball

- A tennis ball lies in the drive before the kennel's mouth, beside the dog's bowl. It is the dog's, and there is no dog.
- It is picked up by walking over it, as anything dropped is. A reader doing June's second job (Biscuit's water) walks over it on the way to the bowl. The first time a reader has it they are told: "A tennis ball, in the drive by the kennel. Its felt is new. Nothing has had it in its mouth."
- Used (right-click with it in the hand), it is thrown the way the reader looks: flat for a level look, a lob for a look up.
- It bounces off whatever it meets and lies where it stops, to be walked over again. It is felt and not rubber: it keeps half of a bounce and rolls a few blocks. It has its own sound.
- It hurts nobody. It bounces off people.

### Callum

Callum does not go out (his shoes are lined up at the door), does not speak, and "copies the older sibling's movements a beat late". So the game is in the hall, where he stands at the far end looking along it, and it is the same thing.

- **When he plays.** While the house waits on its hour, standing where he stands. Not at the table, not asleep, not walking somewhere, not at night.
- **Held out to him**, the ball in a reader's hand and that hand on him: "He looks at the ball, then a long way down the hall, then at you." When he may not play: "He looks at the ball. His hands stay at his sides."
- **Thrown to him.** His hands come up in front of him while it comes his way. If it comes within his reach before it has met a floor or a wall, he has it. He does not take a step for it.
- **He holds it a beat** (twelve ticks: the beat by which he copies a reader out on the line at night).
- **He throws it back the way it came:** to where the reader's hand was when it left it, in the time the reader's throw took to reach him. A flat hard throw comes back flat and hard, a lob comes back a lob.
- **A reader still standing there** has it in their hand again: "It comes back the way you threw it, a moment late."
- **A reader who has moved** watches it come down where they stood: "It comes down where you were standing." It bounces on and lies where it stops.
- **He never goes after it.** A ball thrown past him, or short, or anywhere else in the house while he could have played: "He watches it go by. He does not go after it."
- **He never looks at it.** He watches the reader, as he always does. At the fifth there-and-back without a miss: "He has not looked at the ball once."
- After the second catch: "The ball is wet where his hands were."
- At ten there-and-backs, and every fifth after: "Ten times there and back."

A catch is a catch on the full. A ball that has bounced is nobody's, his or the reader's: it goes where it goes and is picked up off the floor.

### June, and Tilly

- **June**, at her stove in the afternoon, the first time a reader's ball comes down in her house: "Mind the photographs, love." Once a visit.
- **Tilly** can be told of it. To her asking, a reader who has had the ball back from him can say: "Callum plays catch. It comes back just the way I throw it." She answers: "He copies. He does my cane, out in the hall. I've heard him."

### The house keeps its ball

- There is one ball to a house. Two readers in one house share it; each pair's house has its own.
- A reader who leaves the house has it taken back.
- A ball gone where nobody can get it (in the pool's yard with the gate locked, up on a roof, off down the street) is gone from there after a quarter of a minute, once nobody is looking at it, and is in the drive by the kennel again the first moment nobody is looking there.
- When the visit begins again the ball is by the kennel.
- What a reader keeps: how many of his throws they caught, and the most there-and-backs they kept going.

### The hour

- With the ball in his hands, Callum's errands and June's call wait for him to throw it: twelve ticks at most.
- Once the hour has come he does not catch. A ball in the air then comes down.

## Taking Tilly round the house by the arm

- **Offered** as one more answer to Tilly's asking what things look like, in Nessa's room: "Come on. Take my arm and I'll show you." It is offered whenever she is there to be asked, nobody is reading to her, and nobody has her arm.
- **She takes it.** The first time in each of the house's waits she says, on the first visit: "Not too fast. Tell me when there's a step." A week in: "I know the hall now. Fourteen steps. Take me somewhere I don't." After that the reader is only told: "Tilly takes your arm."
- **She goes where the reader goes**, a step behind, her left hand up at their arm and her cane down ahead of her in her right. Through the house, out of its doors, anywhere on June's lot. When the reader stops she stops beside them.
- **Where she will not go:** off June's lot, or into the pool's water. She stops at the edge and keeps hold: "Tilly stops, and keeps hold of your arm. She will not go further this way." By day the painted line does not stop her (June takes her out to the front bed herself); the lot's edge does.
- **Her hand goes where the reader's does.** Whatever of the house's things the reader puts a hand on with her beside them, she turns to and puts her own hand on. Of seventeen kinds of thing she has a word, once a visit each, by touch, smell and what she hears. She says what her hand finds and nothing of what it means. The words are in the table below.
- **It ends**
  - with the reader's hand on her: "She lets go of your arm, and goes back the way you came with her cane ahead of her.";
  - when the reader goes off without her (seven blocks away for three seconds, or out of the house's story): "Tilly has let go of your arm.";
  - when the hour comes: "Tilly lets go of your arm.", and she says "June'll call any minute. I'd better be where she left me." A walk begun late has forty seconds before the hour ends it.
- **She goes back by herself**, by the way she came, opening what is shut in front of her, to where she stood in Nessa's room. June's call to dinner and to bed wait until she is there.
- **Asked again.** The reader's next hand on her has her asking again, and the arm is offered again.
- **One reader at a time.** Another reader's hand on her while she has an arm: "She has somebody's arm." With a reader as Tilly there is nobody to lead, and nothing is offered.

## How it is built

- `PellCatch`: the ball, Callum's catching and throwing, June's word, and the keeping of the one ball. `PellBallItem` is the ball in a hand. The ball in the air is the Polybius prize's `PrizeBall` with this item in it, which now takes its bounce (`PellCatch.BOUNCE`, `ROLL`) and its sound from what it carries.
- `PellArm`: Tilly on a reader's arm, her way back, and her hand on things (`PellHouse.thing` calls `PellArm.touched` after the thing has answered the reader).
- `PellCompany`: the reply "Come on." on Tilly's panel, and the catch as something to tell her (bit 11 the first week, 22 a week in).
- `PellIdle.home` is false while Tilly has an arm or is on her way back, and while Callum has the ball in his hands, so June's call waits; their errands are skipped meanwhile.
- `PellPeople`: three gestures (`ARM`, `CATCH`, `THROW`), posed in `LiteraryRenderers.pell`; the household's state for both.
- Art and sound from the Pell house's own tools: `tools/draw_pell_people.py` (the ball: one 16-pixel texture and a flat model), `tools/generate_pell_audio.py` (three takes of the bounce).
- His throw is worked out, not guessed: `PellCatch.lob` solves the game's own rule for a thrown thing (each tick it moves by its speed, its speed is scaled by 0.99 and pulled down by 0.03) for the speed that lands it on a point in a given number of ticks, and walks that path through the house's collision. If the way is blocked a flatter, quicker throw is tried; if none is clear he rolls it along the floor.
