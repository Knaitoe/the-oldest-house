# This House Has Ghosts in It — a close reading of the prose

*Story and ending spoilers. I read the English game text on [main at 0.4.721](https://github.com/Knaitoe/the-oldest-house/tree/f9f4fa19884023804bca00d3d800fe5988be5530), following the dialogue, books, and the choices that select their lines. This is a selection of close readings from the source. I haven’t playtested their timing or delivery.*

The line that stayed with me was Kendra’s. On a later visit to Borrasca she says, “Nobody ever got over him on the Deep Seam. He'd want me to say so.” On the earlier visit Patrick insists you can hop the timbers. You can watch him try. Kendra can hear him hit one from the fishing cabinet. When she defends his score later, she knows exactly what that score is worth. I believe she misses him because she’s willing to let him have the last word on a stupid argument. I’d keep every part of that exchange, especially the fishing game. Kendra gets to do something she likes while Patrick makes a fool of himself. [The afternoon at the cabinets][arcade] · [Kendra on the next visit][arcade-return]

Kendra sets a high bar for the books the House asks me to sign. When even the arcade remembers what I did for a friend, I expect a book with my name on the cover to remember whether I asked a girl a question.

## Four changes I’d make first

### Mr. Harrigan — let the phone be cold for one visit

Finding him dead and picking up his phone gives me “His phone is cold.” That’s the moment I can feel in my hand. In the same pickup I’m told I can bury it, which phone I might reach him on, and which button to press at the casket. The casket is in a later visit. By the time I should be deciding whether there is something of his I want to keep, I’m already comparing what the two phones do. [Finding him][harrigan-find] · [Picking up the phone][harrigan-take]

I’d end the pickup on the cold phone. Keep the information that he has died and where the phone is. At the funeral, give the choice once, while I can act on it. The cue could read, *Use his phone on the casket to leave it with him, or take it home. If he has his phone, you may be able to call him.* On the keepsake route he can later text, “Turn a light on. You've been sitting in the dark again.” I’d protect that *again*. He talks as if he still has a chair in the room with you. [The funeral cue][harrigan-funeral] · [The later texts][harrigan-kind]

### The House on the Corner — give Romina the right to keep a story

Romina says a dog ran off with her arm. Another time she says her brother got it and now has three. If you share her loss, she can tell you about the fever and dismiss that story as boring. I loved her for that judgment. She’s spent time making up better ones. Even the teacher reading her exercise book has an opinion about which story she should tell. [Romina’s stories and composition book][corner-stories]

The signed account always says “every time I asked about the other one she told me something different.” It can follow that immediately with “I never asked her about it.” If the fever is the only story you heard, the account can say you never asked and then recount the fever. It can also finish a visit where you heard nothing by weighing which of her stories was true. The account already takes care over whether you held her hand. Give her conversation the same care. [What she actually tells you][corner-talk] · [The signed account][corner-book]

I’d make *A girl from the curb took me in. She had one arm.* the common opening. If you heard one story, print it. If you heard several, let the remembered versions disagree on the page. If you never asked, keep “I never asked her about it” and finish on the overgrown path. When she told you the fever story, keep her verdict that it was boring. These versions leave room for two different intimacies. One player heard something she rarely shares. Another held her hand without asking her to explain herself.

### The List — put the names back into the last page

Mel at the party wants something good for the camera. From outside the shut door she tells you to hold still because she can’t get anything. Amber, meanwhile, includes the invitation rule in her notebook even though she knows that one is for vampires. I knew these two apart after a line apiece. One wants footage. The other stayed up doing research, and has written down even the rule she doubts. [Mel at the party and the gin][list-party] · [Amber’s notebook][list-amber]

The four-night account remembers individual names on a partial route. If you kill all five, their names disappear into “I had been to every one of them.” The paragraph already begins “There were five names on the list.” The book ends “The list was five names long.” I felt the repeated count most on the route where the names ought to be hardest to read. [The four-night account][list-book]

I’d keep the evasive *been to* and give it five names to evade. For the full route I’d try *By the last morning I’d been to see Deek and Wes and Mel and Lesley and Shannon.* The repeated *and* makes the speaker say every one. End there if Amber’s rule hasn’t been learned. If it has, let her observation have the last line. On the route where everyone lives, keep the existing image of all five still walking around town. It gives restraint an ending of its own.

### Witness — describe what happened at the cell

“You put the weapon down. Something shifts behind the bars.” This is also what a reader hears after leaving their one thing on the hall stand upstairs, or reaching the cell with no recorded favorite weapon. Both hands have to be empty. Putting something down here is only one way of arriving that way. The account later says, “I can say what I did. I cannot say what the House is.” The ending deserves to keep that promise about the first half. [The conditions and the line at the cell][witness-cell] · [The closing account][witness-book]

I’d use the existing second sentence alone on those two paths. Keep both sentences for the player who actually laid the weapon there. Later, “The stairs lead back. The cell stays open.” is exactly the right level of certainty. I know where I can go and that the creature I passed can go somewhere too. [The stairs][witness-stairs]

## Edits I’d make to individual voices

**Will’s first letter.** “Hope you don't mind” comes after Will has taken the photograph and enclosed a print. That bit of bad manners does more to unsettle me than the flash itself. “Your little window up top was lit” bets the opening on an upstairs window in a home the player built. A player with a windowless base has good reason to study that discrepancy, and the letter offers the identical line with the stock photograph. I’d trade just the window sentence and its follow-up for *I made two prints. Thought you ought to have one.* That gives the reader another reason to turn the photograph over. Will has kept a copy. The postscript about the new measurement can still interrupt his goodbye. [The letter][will-letter] · [The enclosed print][will-photo]

**Speak Up.** The family has declared that I’m fine, that I can’t stay, and that I don’t eat bread before Ruth says, “You can answer.” Later the empty chair can answer in my own words. The special “Say nothing.” choice is even stranger, except that Ruth says “That's an answer too.” twice, once to me and once when the empty chair has its turn. I’d keep her first acknowledgment. At the chair, let Joan ask her question and have the family wait through the space where a reply usually goes. A silent player would get to hear what their silence sounds like from the other side of the table. [The conversation and its echo][speak-table] · [Ruth’s first response][speak-answer]

**The backseat notebook.** The driver is already apologizing to their mother about the fender while the headlights are coming at them. Keep the long sentence long enough to fit that ridiculous, entirely believable priority. Its last clause then identifies what climbed in at the mailbox and explains that it held the wheel steady. The player can read this on the passenger cushion before their own door closes. I’d replace the tail, starting at the glance in the rearview mirror, with a sensation the driver could remember, ending with *and I had both hands over my face when the car eased back into its lane.* The note gives me something dreadful to expect. I still have a reason to turn around. [The notebook and when it can be read][backseat-note] · [The turn that starts the reveal][backseat-turn]

**The Goatman cousins.** The bedtime conversation goes from “What if it comes back?” to a promise to leave at daylight. On the walk out, a cousin claims, “I'm only waiting because you walk so slow.” You can hear somebody who needs company and would die before admitting it. I’d lend that pride to one bedtime exchange. *Wake me if you hear it. Promise?* / *How am I supposed to wake you if you keep talking? We’re going as soon as it’s light.* Then keep “Okay. Leave that light on. Please.” The change gives the children a small irritation they can safely admit to in the middle of a much larger fear. [Bedtime][goat-bed] · [The walk out][goat-walk]

**The other hand in the journal.** Try the sentence *I left Hillary at home.* On an eligible page the word *left* can attract “No. Right.” It’s a striking thing for a stranger to correct my directions in the margin of my journal. Correcting a verb as if it were a direction makes me see the word-matching rule. I’d save the flat contradiction for phrases such as *turned left*. For an ambiguous use, let the margin ask *Left?* In this example that one word could mean the other writer wants to know how I could have left her there. [How the margin chooses its words][journal]

## Letters and found pages

**Ansel’s letters.** In *The laugh*, Ansel has made a list of humiliations. They’ve forgotten who was there and, twice, what the other person said. “Your laugh is very clear.” I’d end the letter there. The existing last sentence explains how much work Ansel has done with so little material. The blank spaces have already shown me the work. Save the polished last word for *The reference*, where Ansel writes an honest recommendation and spends the evening “trying to forgive you for making it so easy.” That turn deserves to catch me unprepared. [The laugh][ansel-laugh] · [The reference][ansel-reference]

**The staircase household.** In *The last plate*, try reading straight from “We left the cracked plate until last” to “She put it under the geranium.” I’d cut the intervening thought about deciding where it belonged finishing the evening. Ruth has found a use for the plate. I can decide what that habit tells me about her. The same collection has “We stood there opening it again” after Jonah fixes a sticky drawer. That is the one I’d use to judge the edit by. There’s pleasure in the extra sentence because two people actually do something quite unnecessary together. [The plate and the repaired drawer][stair-notes]

**Third Floor Girls’.** In the author’s folded note, “your own nose starts looking like belongs to somebody else” reads as though *it* dropped out after *like*. I’d restore that word and keep the headlong sentence around it. The threat to make you go back in gives the breathless instructions a speaker on the other side of the door. [The folded note][dare-note]

## Three passages I’d protect

- **June and Gail at the Pell house.** When you’re Tilly, you can overhear June say, “Tilly's in Nessa's bed. It fits her, Gail.” June evaluates the fit of a bed as if that settles which child belongs in it. On one departure Gail says, “Tilly's settled.” I’d keep that word in Gail’s mouth. By then you know what it costs for an adult to describe a placement as a success. [June on the phone][pell-phone] · [Gail at the car][pell-car]
- **The night worker’s occurrence book.** The supervisor tells D. V. to keep personal observations out. When the baby laughs at a spoon over the phone, D. V. records it as an occurrence. Then the last entry asks a favor. “If you sign it, please tell Maren I went home.” After the call about the spoon, I know who will be listening for the door. I’d keep the bureaucratic formality all the way through. D. V. has earned the right to use the book for that. [The memo][shift-memo] · [The occurrence][shift-spoon] · [The sign-out][shift-signout]
- **The Whistlers in the Pines.** The binder says, “There are two. There are not three.” It means repair tarps. The account puts you in a storm with three torn blinds. This is one place where telling the player the exact inventory is excellent horror writing. It made me wonder who had signed off on sending two. Later, “FOUR OPEN THE HATCH. I HAVE COFFEE.” sounds, for a second, like something a coworker might genuinely have thought to bring. [The binder][lookout-binder] · [Seven’s transmissions][lookout-tape] · [The storm entry][lookout-log]
