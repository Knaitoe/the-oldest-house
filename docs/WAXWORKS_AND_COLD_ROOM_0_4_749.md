# The waxworks and the cold room (0.4.749 onward)

The owner, 23 November: "House of wax vignette - wax models slowly coming to life. At the edge of his vision, the figures head seemed
to move almost like it was trying to get a better look at him. When he sees a figure blink, is it wax come to life or a stranger broke
in? Or only his imagination. Figure only blinks after player has focused on wax figure's face for several seconds. Character(s)
equipped with flashlight in an otherwise dark space. FNF style central security room with camera display for various rooms, watching
for irregularities. Wax figure mimics player movements," and "one based off of the exorcist". "Both should follow all prior tests for
single and multiplayer play."

Both are built in parts, as every recent vignette has been: the place first (never dealt, no Witness source), then the story, then the
account. Each part runs the full multiplayer set (the group sweep, the cast and together checks) as soon as the place exists.

## 1. The waxworks (`waxworks`, slot 247)

A small-town wax museum after closing. The reader is the night guard on their first shift. Kind: survival.

### The place

- **The staff corridor** from the House's door, with the time clock and the guard's locker (the flashlight hangs inside it).
- **The security office**: a desk, a bank of six monitors, the shift log, a desk phone and a wall clock reading from 11:52.
- **Five galleries** off a ring corridor, each with a camera in a corner:
  - the foyer and ticket booth;
  - the Hall of Statesmen;
  - the kitchen tableau (a family at supper);
  - the chamber (a horror tableau behind a rope);
  - the workshop, with vats, heads on shelves and a figure half made.
- **The dark**: there are no lights but the monitors and the flashlight. Shut off and silent, every gallery is too dark to see a face.
  The flashlight is a real moving light, the one the Last Stop's men carry, and it reaches about nine blocks.

### The figures

There are twenty-three figures on the plaques. They are actors (stranger phases), painted as wax: glossy, eyes too clear.

- **The look.** A figure at the edge of a reader's view, between about 35° and 70° off where they look, turns its head toward them a
  little at a time. It stops as soon as anyone looks straight at it. Whatever has turned stays turned, so a reader who looks back
  sees the head facing somewhere it was not.
- **The blink.** When a reader holds the beam on a figure's face, near enough and straight on, for about four seconds, the figure
  blinks once, for that reader alone. A reader standing beside them, looking elsewhere, sees nothing.
- **The mirror.** One figure in the Hall of Statesmen copies the reader nearest it while they face it: head, arms and crouch, a beat
  late and mirrored. It stops the moment the reader looks away.
- **The twenty-fourth.** The cameras count twenty-four. One of them stands where no plaque is, and is not on the plaque list. Whether
  it is wax, a man who came in, or nothing at all is never said.

### The shift

- **The clock.** One shared clock runs while anyone is in the museum, from 11:52 to six. That is twelve minutes of play: two minutes
  an hour.
- **The rounds.** Each hour the log asks for a round: walk to the five galleries and count. A reader who counts a gallery with the
  beam on its figures writes the count in the log.
- **The monitors.** A hand on the bank shows the six feeds as screens drawn from the museum itself: the CCTV renderer the bedroom
  already uses. A feed shows what its camera sees, other readers included.
- **Irregularities.** While no reader is looking and no feed shows the place, a figure may move off its mark, step closer to the office,
  or be gone from its plinth. A reader at the monitors who notes it ("Something's moved" on that feed) logs it. An irregularity nobody
  notices within the hour comes one gallery nearer the office.
- **Failure.** When three unnoticed irregularities reach the office, the monitors go to snow and a figure stands in the office doorway.
  The reader's light dies. They wake in the House, unfinished. The figure never harms anyone.
- **Success.** A reader still on shift at six, with every hour's round in the log, has finished. The day man's key turns in the front
  door, and the reader finds the log on the desk: the account.

### More than one reader

- **A party of four.** One copy of the museum holds up to four readers, each with a flashlight of their own from the locker. Anyone can
  take the office.
- **What is shared.** The clock, the irregularities, the count and the log are shared.
- **What is personal.** The blink, the look and the mirror belong to each reader: each sees their own, and the account tells only what
  that reader saw.
- **Working together.** A reader in the office can talk a reader on rounds to a feed they cannot see. The game does not do this for
  them; it is just what the cameras make possible.

### The account

The account is the night guard's log, in the reader's hand. It records:

- the counts the reader wrote, and whether they found twenty-four;
- each blink they saw, and on which figure;
- whether the mirror copied them;
- whether they called the police from the desk phone.

If they called, the men came at five, walked the galleries with the lights up, counted twenty-three and left. The log never says
what the twenty-fourth was.

## 2. The cold room (`cold_room`, slot 248)

The shape of the book and the film, with every name, line and date the House's own. A rented house on a steep street in winter: a
mother, her daughter in the back bedroom, and an old priest who has come for the last time. The readers come as the ones he sent for.
Kind: understanding.

### The place

- **The front hall and the stair.**
- **The kitchen**, where the mother sits.
- **The landing.**
- **The back bedroom**: the bed with its restraints, a dresser with a crucifix, a window over the long outside steps, and a radiator
  that does nothing.

The room is cold, and colder as the night goes on: breath shows, frost climbs the glass, and the cold hurts without ever taking the
last three hearts.

### The girl

The girl is one actor in the bed, painted for the book's sickness: drawn and scabbed, never gory. She is a child, so nothing done to
her is shown, and nothing she says is cruelty about her body.

- **The voice.** She speaks in the voice of the House, and it knows the reader. It uses only what the House has really kept of them:
  their name, the companions they lost (native pet-death records, as the monkey's paw uses them), their deaths, what they wrote in
  their own journal. It never invents a history.
- **The bed.** It shakes, rises a little and comes down. The dresser drawers slide out on their own. The crucifix turns on its nail.
  All of it happens only while nobody is looking at it.

### The rite

The rite is read from the old priest's book on the dresser.

- **Reading.** A reader with the book open and reading turns a page for every line read. The rite is twelve pages.
- **Interruption.** When she speaks to a reader, that reader's reading stops until they look back at the page.
- **The cold.** The cold rises while nobody is reading.
- **The water.** A reader can sprinkle holy water from the flask on the dresser. It quiets her for a few seconds, and the flask holds
  six.
- **Holding her.** A second reader can hold her down at the restraints, and while she is held the bed does not rise. A reader alone
  reads and sprinkles in turn.

### The offer

At the ninth page the voice offers the reader something real. If they lost a companion, it is that companion: "He is here with me.
Let me out of her and you can have him back." Otherwise it offers a death undone. The offer comes up as a choice on the conversation
panel:

- **Answer it** ("Where is he?"): the reading stops for good for that reader. The voice laughs. The reader is sent down the stairs and
  out, unfinished. Nothing is given back.
- **Keep reading**: the page turns.

### The old priest

The old priest kneels by the bed and reads with the readers. At the eleventh page his heart gives out. He is found sitting against the
bed with the book in his lap.

### The end

The last page is not in the book. A reader standing over the girl is offered, by the panel, the line the book never prints: "Take me
instead."

- **One reader says it.** The voice leaves her. That reader's view goes white, and they are at the window. Then they are on the long
  steps outside, at the bottom, alive. They have finished, and so has every reader in the room.
- **Nobody says it.** At dawn she sleeps, the cold lifts a little, and the mother comes up the stairs. It is over, and nobody knows for
  how long. The readers have finished too, with a different account.

### More than one reader

- **A party of three.** One copy of the house holds up to three readers.
- **What is shared.** The pages read, the cold, the water and the old priest.
- **What is personal.** The voice speaks to one reader at a time. What it says, and whether they answered the offer, is each reader's
  own.
- **The end.** The one who says the line is the one taken to the window. The account tells each reader what they did: who read, who
  held her, whose voice they heard.
