# Johnny Truant

Resident Haunter — Design Draft

Project: This House Has Ghosts in It
Date: 2 November 2026
Status: Creative design draft. No implementation is included.

(Recorded verbatim from the owner, second revision, 2 November 2026. Tables are kept as two-column lists.)

## 1. The central idea

Truant is someone the player becomes glad to find in the House.

He knows a sheltered recess, a useful door, a way around a dangerous passage. He holds a light while the player gets through. His help is real.

He asks to hear what the player has experienced. As his appetite grows, a conversation becomes an obligation in his mind. He begins claiming that his help entitled him to more than the player ever offered.

Eventually, the person who steadied the player's back at a narrow crossing uses the same hand to push them from a ledge.

He inhabits the labyrinth alongside its other residents. His encounters arise during exploration, particularly in cramped, dark spaces. His presence should make the player reconsider places they have previously associated with relief.

The defining tension is that he can remain useful while becoming personally dangerous.

That usefulness persists. Even after a violent encounter, he can offer genuine help, keep a promise, or let someone rest without demanding anything. These moments need enough substance that accepting his company can remain a reasonable choice.

In multiplayer, everyone sees the same person, but each player may have a different history with him. The group can agree about what he has done and still disagree about whether to trust his next offer.

## 2. Established direction and working proposals

| Established direction | Meaning for the design |
|---|---|
| A resident of the House | He exists within ordinary exploration, with places he occupies and moves between. |
| Cramped, dark spaces are his primary territory | Corners, recesses, service passages, low ceilings, narrow stairs, and ledges shape his encounters. |
| Helpful meetings can occur earlier | The player has opportunities to know and trust him before violence becomes possible. |
| Helpfulness remains possible after violence | His deterioration never settles into permanent, universal hostility. Later kindness can be genuine. |
| Shared visibility, different relationships | Everyone sees the same Truant; experiences, promises, and interpretations can differ between players. |
| Hostile collection begins no earlier than twenty passes | Twenty unlocks the possibility of an attack. It does not require a transformation on that exact crossing. |
| His addiction and deterioration intensify | His need becomes more demanding, his explanations less reliable, and his behavior more intrusive. |
| Temporary reprieve carries a greater cost | He gradually seeks dangerous favors and control over the player's choices. |
| Half his face leering around a corner | A central recurring image, with a visible human expression. |
| A shove from a ledge, from behind | A rare, deliberate act of physical malice, capable of serious injury or death. |
| He should not behave like a typical Minecraft stalker | His presence and violence develop through familiarity, confinement, bargaining, and betrayal. |

Working proposals in this draft: his addiction is focused on accounts of the House; his demands move from hearing stories to making the player produce new experiences; his human vulnerability remains visible; attacks are brief and situational.

The precise nature of his addiction, ultimate mortality, and highest possible demand remain open for further design.

## 3. Relationship to House of Leaves

The working identity is Johnny Truant. This is the mod's interpretation of him as a resident and potential aggressor.

The source foundation is his consuming involvement with Zampanò's manuscript, his intrusive contribution to its narration, and the instability around the account he presents. The compulsive need for the player's experiences, the debt relationship, and the encounters described here are original adaptations.

Maintain uncertainty around what he understands about the House and about himself. His confident explanation can be wrong. His fear can be genuine even while he is manipulating someone.

His appearance and dialogue should preserve the impression of a person. Fear, exhaustion, calculation, shame, and pleasure can coexist in him.

All sample dialogue in this document is newly written.

## 4. His place in the House

Truant has a small geography of his own: places to sit, squeeze into, work, listen, and withdraw.

Suitable places include:

- the recess behind a partly open service door;
- a tight turn at the bottom of a stair;
- a shallow maintenance alcove with a lamp;
- the inner corner of a narrow landing;
- a low opening between two ordinary passages;
- the dark side of a railing;
- a gap behind exposed pipes or a displaced cabinet.

The player may encounter him already occupying one of these places. He can also be seen moving between them.

His appearances need spatial continuity. If he withdraws around a corner, there should be somewhere for him to go. He can move while watched. Looking directly at him is not a universal command that freezes or removes him.

He does not accompany the player everywhere. His recurring presence is concentrated around particular kinds of space.

His own refuges can become compromised as the relationship worsens. Preserve the established identity of the House's other calm places and authored encounters.

He has no authority over the Minotaur, Navidson's party, or the Mother of Strays. His usefulness comes from experience, local knowledge, and actions he can perform.

## 5. Early meetings and the twenty-pass threshold

### Before twenty

The early meetings establish him as useful and recognizably human.

He might:

- hold a door while the player gets through;
- steady the player at a narrow crossing;
- bring a lamp closer to show the end of a passage;
- point out a recess where the player can briefly stop;
- warn them about something he has actually heard;
- remain nearby without asking for an immediate task.

He can be strange or uncomfortable. The player can catch a contradiction or an unusually hungry interest in their story. However, these meetings do not conceal an early Truant-initiated assault.

The first kindness should stand on its own. The player should have reason to remember that he genuinely helped.

### Twenty and afterward

At twenty passes, hostile collection becomes possible. The next suitable encounter can reveal it.

For pacing, the threshold concerns forward exploration through the House, rather than twenty completed stories. Backtracking after reaching the threshold should not make him forget that the relationship has progressed.

His deterioration can continue after this point. A first threatening encounter does not require every subsequent appearance to become an attack.

Twenty passes opens the possibility of violence. It does not retire the helpful person the player met. He can still help someone he previously threatened, and that person is allowed to remember both acts.

Someone who declined his help can still meet him as a resident. His claim of a personal debt should reflect only what actually happened, or be clearly recognizable as his accusation.

The reader should not receive an announcement that a new enemy tier has unlocked.

## 6. The addiction

### Working interpretation: he needs another account

At first, hearing about the player's experience is enough.

He asks what was in a room, what someone said, or how the player got out. He can ask a specific follow-up that reveals he was listening.

Then an account becomes insufficient. He wants more detail, more certainty, or a different ending. He begins behaving as though a story can provide something he cannot obtain himself.

Eventually he wants the player to go somewhere frightening and return with a new experience for him.

He is drawn toward the House's stories and afraid of undergoing them. The player becomes someone he can send ahead.

### What deterioration looks like

Show changes across meetings:

- He repeats a question that has already been answered.
- He begins an anecdote in the player's words.
- He remembers an offered kindness more clearly than the promise he made afterward.
- He becomes irritated when the player leaves an account incomplete.
- He neglects his own things while repeatedly working over a page.
- He treats a reasonable refusal as the withholding of something necessary.
- He is briefly lucid about his behavior, then reconstructs a justification.

His cruelty should also have moments of intention. The leer matters because the player can see that he sometimes enjoys their vulnerability.

His expression should not be explained away every time as an accident of fear.

## 7. Reprieve, payment, and the invented debt

### What he actually gives

The reprieve should be tangible and limited.

He offers a sheltered stopping place, holds a barrier, lends useful light, or identifies a route that avoids an immediate danger. He is doing something the player can recognize.

The relief does not have to include healing or a mechanical buff. Being able to breathe, look around, and speak without an imminent threat can be valuable.

He can offer the first instance freely. Later he begins describing that kindness as a loan.

### What the player actually promises

Early exchanges are simple:

> “When you get through, tell me what was in there.”

The player can agree, qualify the promise, refuse, or return voluntarily later.

A verbal account is sufficient. The relationship must work for someone who does not keep a journal.

If the player chooses to show or give him an account, he can make a copy for his own papers. The player's authored writing and earned story record remain their own.

### What he later claims

Keep three things distinct:

1. What he did for the player.
2. What the player actually agreed to do.
3. What he now claims the agreement entitled him to.

The difference gives the relationship its conflict.

A player can have fulfilled their promise and still face an escalating demand. Truant believes he has found an unpaid part of it.

> “You told me how you got out. You didn't tell me what you left behind.”

His insistence is personal. The game does not certify his interpretation as a binding supernatural contract.

### The greater price

The proposed greater price is the player's increasing dependence on him and willingness to undertake danger on his behalf.

Possible demands include:

- open the hatch he will not approach;
- go first through an unlit gap;
- put a light farther into a space where it cannot be kept close;
- extinguish a lamp so he can inspect the darkness beyond it;
- rub out a navigation mark he claims is attracting something;
- revisit a passage the player escaped and examine what they chose to leave alone.

The consequences should follow the act. Removing a mark makes the return less secure. Going first places the player ahead of his protection. Moving the lamp changes what they can see.

The most serious favors should be recognizable as escalations. They are proposed encounters, not an automatic tax on every interaction.

Do not spend another player's belongings or commitments through someone else's agreement.

## 8. The relationship across encounters

| Aspect | What the player encounters | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| A useful stranger | Real help, a sheltered pause, a conversation | The player learns his face, voice, and gestures. |
| An interested listener | Specific questions and small requests | A shared history develops. |
| A claimant | Repeated demands, borrowed phrases, an inflated account of his help | The player realizes he is keeping a different version of the relationship. |
| A collector | Bottlenecks, withheld passage, coercion, and possible violence | Familiarity itself becomes threatening. |

These aspects can coexist. Early meetings emphasize the useful stranger and listener; later encounters can draw on all four. Different members of a party may have encountered different sides of him. They should not become four compulsory missions in a fixed room or a progression into permanent aggression.

He can still help after becoming dangerous. A useful warning or a moment of restraint leaves the player unsure whether to approach the lamp next time.

He can also choose malice while speaking with the familiarity of an earlier meeting.

## 9. Defining image: the half-hidden face

The player rounds a bend and sees part of a face beyond the next corner.

One eye, part of the nose, and half the mouth are visible. The rest of Truant remains concealed behind the wall or jamb.

His expression is a tight, unpleasant smile. It suggests that he knows he has been noticed and is enjoying the moment.

Let the image remain long enough to be recognized.

Possible variations:

- He slowly draws his head back.
- He leans farther out, revealing that he is closer than expected.
- He stays still while his expression changes.
- He speaks from that position without showing the rest of himself.
- He withdraws into a recess the player can subsequently inspect.

Looking at him does not automatically end the encounter.

If the player approaches, the body should still make sense in the space. Finding him pressed sideways into a shallow recess can be worse than finding nothing.

### Appearance

Keep the face recognizably human and consistent with the mod's adult character proportions.

Proposed details are tired skin, a worn expression, small asymmetries, and traces of ink on his hands. The illuminated portion of the face takes ordinary light. The rest is genuinely in shadow.

Use restrained changes in the eyes and mouth. The leer needs to remain disturbing at the game's pixel scale.

### Sound

The first indication can be close breathing, a small scrape, or a voice from the bend. Many appearances can be quiet enough that the player notices the face first.

Do not attach the same loud sting to every appearance. Recognition should carry some of the fear.

## 10. Defining attack: the shove

Truant comes up behind a player near a ledge and pushes them.

This is a rare, deliberate attack with the potential to injure or kill. Its cruelty comes partly from the familiarity of the contact.

### The reversal

In an early encounter, he steadies the player's back while they negotiate a narrow crossing.

In a later encounter, the hand returns to the same place and shoves.

The gesture should be recognizable without a speech explaining the parallel.

### The approach

He needs a plausible route to the player's back: a side passage, an adjacent landing, or the dark portion of the same balcony.

A short cue may reveal his presence: a shoe scraping behind the player, a breath close to their ear, or a witness calling out.

The approach gives the player a chance to turn, move aside, or confront him. It should also remain frighteningly brief.

### The act

He plants his hands between the player's shoulders and commits to the push.

The fall follows the place. A lower landing can mean injury, separation, and a difficult route back. A deep shaft can mean death.

Do not automatically convert every shove into a harmless scripted landing. Equally, the encounter should not begin with an unseen force moving a player who has no control.

A friend can witness him approach, push, and look over the edge.

### Afterward

He does not need to chase the falling player.

He may look down, breathe hard, say something familiar, or withdraw into the passage he used.

A particularly cold possibility is one word:

> “Careful.”

A shove should remain exceptional. Repeatedly attempting it at every railing would make him predictable.

## 11. Other encounter forms

### The hand in the doorway

He once held the door open. Now he occupies its narrow opening and asks for something before letting the player pass.

The encounter can remain conversational, end with him stepping aside, or become a short physical struggle.

His stance, expression, and hand on the frame communicate the change before an attack.

### The covered light

At close range, he reaches across the player's light or interferes with the lamp beside them.

The darkness is temporary and local. His body is still present.

He can use the brief obscurity to move across the player's path or catch their wrist. The player retains ordinary ways to act.

### The collector's question

The player finds his familiar lamp in an alcove. He asks about a particular experience they have already had.

A correct account may satisfy him for that meeting. It may also expose that he is asking for something he has already received.

The important choice is how the player responds to his claim. This is not a memory quiz that kills someone for forgetting a detail.

### Help after harm

After a threatening meeting, he helps someone through an actual danger or makes room at his lamp without demanding payment. The relief is real and can end there.

He can admit that he went too far, keep a promise, stop himself from touching the player, or simply sit with them. Some helpful encounters have no concealed attack or deferred charge.

His helpfulness can cost him effort, exposure, or the chance to obtain something he wanted. This gives the player a reason to take the act seriously.

The new kindness and the earlier harm both remain part of the relationship. Neither overwrites the other.

## 12. Player agency and responses

The player can:

- accept or refuse an offered reprieve;
- tell an account, decline to discuss it, or challenge his version;
- set a limit on a promise;
- undertake or reject a dangerous favor;
- leave a conversation;
- back away from an occupied passage;
- use light and wider space to change an encounter;
- interrupt his approach;
- resist or strike him during an assault.

His responses can be unfair or malicious, but the record of what the player did should remain accurate.

He does not need a boss fight or a universal gaze rule. He can be physically hurt and driven back. The final decision about whether his death is permanent remains open.

Violence should be short and personal. An encounter can end with separation, retreat, a narrowly escaped fall, or his choice to release the player.

Do not require the reader to consume a long piece of text while an attack is underway.

## 13. Shared encounters in multiplayer

Everyone present sees the same Truant and the same physical act.

### Different histories with one person

His multiplayer appeal comes from a group forming different, defensible judgments about the same resident.

| Player's actual experience | A reasonable interpretation |
|---|---|
| He held a door during a pursuit, waited, and asked for nothing. | He can be trusted to help when it matters. |
| He blocked a passage, invented a debt, or pushed them from a ledge. | Getting close to him is dangerous. |
| They know about the assault, but he has just helped their injured friend. | His help may still be worth accepting, with caution. |

These are possible player interpretations, not labels the game assigns to them. The player defending him should have genuine experience to draw on. Believing he can help does not require being ignorant of everything he has done.

One player may have met him while a friend was elsewhere. Another may have witnessed an assault from an angle the target could not see. Those different experiences give the group something to discuss.

The same meeting can also support different judgments: he may recognize one reader warmly, provide real assistance, and address another with a possessive demand. Everyone present can observe those actions.

### A shared encounter

A party finds his familiar lamp at a narrow turn. One player wants to approach because he previously helped them. Another hangs back because of the ledge.

Truant remembers the first player's story and moves the lamp so their injured companion can get through. He waits until they are clear. Then he asks the second player for something they never promised him.

The help has happened. So has the demand. The group must decide how to respond to both.

Alternatively, the meeting can end after the help, with no collection at all. Continued nonhostile encounters are part of his character, including late in exploration.

### Individual relationships and group consequences

A shove can have a witness. A friend can interrupt his approach or help create room to get past him. Two people can disagree about whether his current offer is worth accepting.

Promises remain attributable to their speakers. Someone who accepted his help cannot volunteer another person's body, journal, pet, or possessions as payment.

His claimed grievance can be addressed to one reader without creating contradictory versions of the room for everyone else.

Helping one participant does not automatically settle another participant's dispute. Likewise, a refusal or quarrel with one person does not automatically make every member of the party his permanent enemy.

His attitude can reflect whom he recognizes, what he wants, what he believes he is owed, what just happened, and whether he is afraid. The uncertainty should have character and context behind it.

Do not turn a friendly relationship into immunity. The person he likes can still become a target. They also retain a reason to hope he will choose restraint.

Recommended gate policy: an early reader standing with a veteran should not receive a Truant-initiated lethal encounter before reaching twenty passes. During such mixed encounters, he can remain helpful, demanding, or unsettling without attacking.

This preserves a shared resident while protecting the intended progression of the relationship.

Do not reproduce players' chat or voice as though Truant were one of them. His accusations and quotations remain attributable to him.

## 14. Relationship to journals, memory, and other residents

Truant can quote an account the player explicitly shared, refer to something witnessed together, or describe an experience as his own.

His papers can expose that appropriation. A reader may recognize their own act in his first-person retelling.

Keep the original player text, its authorship, and the actual history intact. His claim is part of the fiction, not an instruction to destroy the player's writing or revoke earned progress.

The existing journal co-author can remain ambiguous. Do not retroactively identify every earlier annotation as Truant, especially those occurring before his introduction.

His identity should complement the House's existing Truant-related material without turning every associated room into one of his ambushes.

Other residents retain their own purposes. He should not casually command them, replace them, or use them to enforce an account's supposed debt.

Seeing him is not treated as completing a vignette. Any separate written account or story resolution associated with him needs its own narrative justification.

## 15. Tone and dialogue

Early speech is useful and human. Later speech becomes possessive, repetitive, intrusive, and occasionally cruel.

He can still speak quietly during a violent phase. Constant shouting would erase the familiarity.

Possible original lines:

Offering help

> “Here. There's room if you turn sideways.”
> “I'll hold it. Get through.”
> “Stay a minute. Listen before you go.”

Asking for an account

> “What did you find?”
> “Start before that. You skipped something.”
> “Tell me what happened after the light went.”

Claiming more

> “You said you'd come back.”
> “I didn't ask for the part you could tell anybody.”
> “I got you through, didn't I?”

Collecting

> “You first. Then tell me.”
> “I haven't had the ending.”
> “You still have something of mine.”

A claimed promise must either match something the reader actually said or be presented as Truant's allegation. Do not make the narration falsely validate him.

His need can be desperate. His pleasure in another person's fear can also be deliberate.

## 16. Encounter sketches

### A. First shelter

The player hears movement in a tight passage and finds Truant holding a door against its own weight.

He helps them through and points to a recessed lamp. He asks nothing until the immediate danger has passed.

He wants to know what was behind them.

The player can answer and leave having received genuine help.

### B. A story that has changed hands

On a later meeting, he is working over a page. The reader recognizes the experience being described, but Truant is telling it in the first person.

He reacts to correction as though the player is being possessive about something he helped make possible.

He still offers a useful route. Accepting it now has a different emotional meaning.

### C. Half a smile

After twenty passes, the player sees half his face around a low corner.

He waits until they recognize him. His mouth moves into a smile. He asks for the rest of an account, then leans farther into the passage.

This can end with a refusal and withdrawal. The uncertainty belongs to the encounter; an attack is not mandatory.

### D. The hand returns

The player pauses on a narrow landing. Truant reaches them from behind.

The contact recalls the earlier crossing. Then he pushes.

If another player is present, they may see enough to warn or interrupt. If the target survives the fall, Truant is visible above for a moment before he leaves.

The event changes what his earlier kindness means without making that kindness unreal.

### E. The friend who knows him differently

After an assault, a player warns their party against approaching his alcove. Another participant recalls a different meeting: Truant stayed to hold a barrier while they got away.

At the next encounter he keeps a small promise to that participant. He may help the whole party, including the person he hurt, and then let them leave.

The players have to carry these events together. The story does not announce which interpretation of him is correct.

## 17. Open design decisions

These choices should remain visible during the next design pass:

- **The addiction's exact nature.** Is the hunger for accounts sufficient on its own, or should substances, sleeplessness, and other aspects of his life play a larger role?
- **His most serious demand.** How far can collection go beyond dangerous favors and interference with navigation?
- **Mortality.** Can a player permanently kill this resident, or can an encounter end only by injury, retreat, or separation?
- **His understanding of the debt.** How often is he knowingly lying, and how often has he come to believe his own version?
- **Identity and uncertainty.** How much should the mod disclose about whether this is Johnny himself, a version of him within the House, or an identity he cannot fully establish?
- **Encounter frequency.** How much ordinary exploration should separate appearances so that recognition remains unsettling?

Exact damage, timing, encounter odds, and other tuning belong to a later pass. This document establishes the character, imagery, relationship, and intended experience.

## 18. Source foundation

- Mark Z. Danielewski, *House of Leaves*.
- Penguin Random House reading guide: Truant's consuming engagement with the manuscript, unreliable accounts, the Minotaur connection, and the distinction between narrative voices.
- LAist interview with Mark Z. Danielewski: typography as a way of identifying the book's different voices.

The encounters, debt structure, dialogue, and progression described in this draft are proposals for the mod.

The defining image is a familiar hand changing its purpose: first keeping a door open for the reader, later closing it—or pressing between their shoulders at the edge of a drop.
